## The transformation: values and prices, 2000–2014

Everything above works with aggregate magnitudes: the total surplus value of a country is the sum of its profits, interest, rents, taxes and unproductive salaries, and at that level the formation of prices changes neither the mass nor the rate of surplus value. But prices are not proportional to values industry by industry: competition between capitals tends to equalise the rates of profit, and in doing so redistributes surplus value from the industries that employ much living labour to those that employ much capital. This section measures that redistribution with the input–output tables of 43 countries (WIOD, 2000–2014) and checks and extends it to 2024 with the OECD world tables. Education and health enter through their capitalist part: the part that is neither public nor non-profit.

### How far do prices depart from values?

For each industry we calculate how many hours of labour, direct and indirect, one dollar of its product contains: the hours worked in the industry itself plus those contained in the inputs it buys from the capitalist part of other productive industries, domestic or imported. If prices were proportional to those hours, all industries would have the same ‘price-value’. The line shows how far, on average, market prices depart from the values so calculated. The deviation is measured as the distance between value and price as a proportion of price: a deviation of 20% means that, on average, the value of what each industry sells is 20% higher or lower than its price (it is not symmetrical: an industry that sells 20% above its value has a deviation of 16.7%).

### Who cedes value and who appropriates it ({{.tf-anio}}, {{.tf-n}} countries)

Price/value below 1: the industry sells below the value it produces and cedes surplus value to other industries. Above 1: it sells above and appropriates surplus value produced elsewhere. Marx expected industries with much capital per worker (high composition) to sell above their value, and those with much living labour, below. The table confirms it: agriculture, fishing, textiles and hotels and restaurants cede value; petroleum refining, mining, energy, metals and chemicals appropriate it.

### To {{.tf-ifin}}, with the OECD world tables

The WIOD tables end in 2014. The OECD world tables (ICIO, 2026 edition) cover 84 economies and twelve rest-of-the-world regions, with the country of origin of each input: the labour contained in what a country imports is calculated with the hours of the country that produced it, not with a world average. The full series 2000–{{.tf-ifin}} is used here, all from the same edition; 2023–2024 are the OECD's own projections. The hours come from OECD employment by industry (TiM), which runs to 2022, and from the Penn World Table hours per person employed. The medians cover only the {{tf-in}} economies with measured employment. The levels are not directly comparable with those of WIOD: there are more countries, in particular low-income ones, a different industry classification and a different treatment of imported inputs. What is compared is the pattern: who cedes value, who receives it and how far price departs from value. One result stands out: China delivered more than 200 billion hours a year through its trade in the mid-2000s and fewer than 80 billion from 2020, while India delivers 112 billion in 2005, 137 billion in 2013, 89 billion in 2016 and 113 billion in 2024. In 2014, the only year in which they can be compared, China delivers 141 billion hours with the OECD tables and 146 billion with WIOD: two different sources give almost the same result.

### The labour hour on the world market (billion hours)

An hour of labour in China does not count the same as one in the United States on the world market: the more productive national labour counts as more intensive labour. The weighted line converts each country's hours of surplus labour into world-market hours, according to how many dollars an hour of labour produces there compared with the average. With that weighting, China's weight in the world mass is much smaller, although it grows rapidly.

### The transformation according to Moseley, with the temporal variant (TSSI) as a contrast

The previous measurement calculates values in hours. This one follows the interpretation of Moseley (2016), which the cognitive map adopts as central: the money costs—inputs and wages—are the same for values and for prices of production, and what changes is only how surplus value is distributed. In values, each industry would have the surplus value produced by its own workers; in prices of production, each industry receives a profit proportional to the capital it advances. The difference is the surplus value that competition shifts from some industries to others. The temporal variant (TSSI; Kliman, 2007) values inputs and capital at the prices at which they entered production: in years of inflation it gives higher rates, and in the 2009 crisis it converges with the central one.

The industries that gain from equalisation are those with much capital per worker—water, audiovisual, electricity and gas, mining, waste, telecommunications—; those that cede surplus value are those with much living labour: professional services, the capitalist part of education and health, computer services and construction. Effective rates are closer to one another than the rates ‘in values’, as the theory expects, although equalisation is partial. On the other hand, market prices are not systematically closer to prices of production than to values: only in {{tf-ppm}} of countries and years. The two measurements do not agree in every industry: agriculture cedes value when measured in hours, but gains from equalisation in Moseley's (see below).

### Why agriculture cedes value in one measurement and gains in the other

There are two reasons. The first is that the two measurements do not answer the same question. The measurement in hours compares market price with value; Moseley's, above all, price of production with value. In Moseley's, agriculture receives surplus value through the equalisation of rates, because its price of production exceeds its value, and its market price ends up somewhat above its value (value/price {{tf-ag-mo}}). The second reason, the decisive one, is the valuation of own-account labour: only {{tf-ag-as}} of those employed in agriculture are wage earners. In hours, each hour of a peasant counts as an hour of social labour; in Moseley's, their labour enters through the money income they receive, which is very low. If only the hours of wage earners are counted in the measurement in hours, the value/price of agriculture falls from {{tf-ag-v1}} to {{tf-ag-v1a}}: wage-labour agriculture sells, on average, close to its value. In China, which accounts for almost 40% of the agricultural output of the 43 countries, WIOD does not separate out employees; the same assumption is used as in the world mass (ILO employees in the non-agricultural industries, none in family agriculture). In the median of countries and years, that valuation explains around {{tf-ag-expl}} of the distance between the two measurements. The rest comes from wages: in hours, an hour is an hour; in Moseley's, it is the wage paid that counts, and agricultural wage earners earn less per hour than the average.
{: .llano }

:::como How are the values and deviations calculated?
1. **Values by industry.** For each country and year, with the WIOD national input–output table (in dollars): λ = (l + (λ̄ ∘ φ) · M) · (I − D<sub>φ</sub> A)<sup>−1</sup>, where D<sub>φ</sub> is the diagonal matrix with the capitalist fraction φ of each supplying industry. l = hours worked per dollar of output (all persons employed, including own-account workers, because they too produce commodities; hours from WIOD SEA; in China, 46 h × 50 weeks); A = domestic inputs per dollar of output; M = imported inputs; λ̄ = average hours contained in one exported dollar of each product in the world (average of the 43 countries weighted by their exports), which is solved by iteration.
2. **Industries that do not produce value.** φ = 1 in the productive industries and 0 in trade, finance, legal and accounting services, advertising, real estate, public administration and households: their inputs do not transfer value; they are expenses paid out of surplus value (the treatment of Shaikh and Tonak, 1994). In education and health, φ = 1 − g, with g the public and non-profit share of employment (the same as in the world mass): only their capitalist part produces value, and it is assumed to have the same cost structure as the industry as a whole.
3. **Price-value.** μ = Σ φ · output / Σ λ · φ · output (dollars per hour, capitalist part of the productive industries); price-value of industry j = μ · λ<sub>j</sub>; price/value = 1 / (μ · λ<sub>j</sub>). Mean deviation = Σ output<sub>j</sub> · |μλ<sub>j</sub> − 1| / Σ output<sub>j</sub>. Correlation: Spearman, across industries, of price/value with K/v (nominal capital stock over labour income, of employees and own-account workers, WIOD SEA).
4. **Trade.** Hours delivered = Σ λ<sub>j</sub> · φ<sub>j</sub> · exports<sub>j</sub>; hours received = Σ λ̄<sub>j</sub> · φ<sub>j</sub> · imports<sub>j</sub> × (exports / imports), so as not to confuse the transfer with the trade deficit. It covers the 43 WIOD countries, not the rest of the world, which is why the figures are smaller than those of the approximation in the countertendencies section, which covers more than a hundred countries.
5. **World weighting.** For each country, μ<sub>c</sub> = GDP in current dollars / hours worked (World Bank and PWT); weight w<sub>c</sub> = μ<sub>c</sub> / mean μ of the productive hours of the 42 countries. Weighted mass = Σ surplus labour<sub>c</sub> × w<sub>c</sub>. The total of hours does not change; it is redistributed according to national productivity on the world market. After the last PWT year the last weight is kept.

6. **OECD world tables (F4 v3, 2000–2024).** With the full ICIO world matrix, 2026 edition, for every year (84 economies, twelve regions and 50 industries; 2023 and 2024 are OECD projections): λ = l · (I − D<sub>φ</sub> A)<sup>−1</sup>, without the average λ̄, because each imported input is valued with the hours of the country that produced it. l = persons employed by industry (OECD, TiM 2025) × annual hours per person employed (PWT) / output. TiM runs to 2022; in 2023 and 2024 the 2022 industry structure is kept and scaled with total PWT employment (2024 = 2023). In professional services (M), φ is the share of architecture and engineering, research and other professional services in the output of M according to WIOD 2014 (excluding legal, accounting and advertising services). For regions without employment data, l is imputed with the world median of hours per dollar of value added of the industry; those regions enter the matrix but not the medians in the chart. It is calculated with `herramientas/icio.py` from the OECD files, which weigh hundreds of MB and do not allow automatic downloads; only the compact results are stored. For 2000–2014 the results can be checked against WIOD; with the 2025 edition, those for 2000–2005 were almost identical (China delivered 233 billion hours in 2005, against 231 with the 2026 edition).
7. **Reconciliation with Moseley's measurement.** Value/price in hours with only the hours of employees: l<sub>j</sub> × (employees<sub>j</sub> / persons employed<sub>j</sub>), WIOD SEA (in China, the ILO share s). It is compared with W<sub>j</sub> / output<sub>j</sub> from Moseley's measurement. Share of the difference explained = (v1 − v1 employees only) / (v1 − Moseley), median of countries and years, in agriculture.

Interpretation: values by industry are not ‘embodied labour’ as a physical property of products, but an approximation to socially necessary labour time as a determination of the social totality. They are calculated simultaneously (values and prices of the same year).

**Moseley and TSSI (F4 v2).** In national currency, by country and year, with the capitalist part of the productive industries (all education and health magnitudes multiplied by φ): c<sub>j</sub> = inputs from productive industries (domestic and imported, from the input–output table converted with WIOD SEA output); v<sub>j</sub> = compensation of employees (in China, which WIOD records without separating out employees, compensation × s, with s the ILO share of employees in the non-agricultural industries and zero in agriculture, as in the world mass); a<sub>j</sub> = labour income of own-account workers (they produce value, not surplus value); K<sub>j</sub> = nominal capital stock. S = Σ (output − c − v − a), which includes the inputs from unproductive industries, paid out of surplus value; e = S / V; r = S / (Σ K + Σ V). Value: W<sub>j</sub> = c<sub>j</sub> + a<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub>(1 + e). Price of production: PP<sub>j</sub> = c<sub>j</sub> + a<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub> + r (K<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub>). Both sums coincide with total output, and total surplus value with total profit. Transfer = (PP<sub>j</sub> − W<sub>j</sub>) / W<sub>j</sub>; share redistributed = Σ |PP − W| / 2 / S. Effective rate = (output − c − v − a) / (K + v); rate in values = e · v / (K + v); dispersion: coefficient of variation weighted by capital. **TSSI:** inputs are deflated with the input price index of each industry (II_PI<sub>t</sub> / II_PI<sub>t−1</sub>) and the capital advanced is the previous year's stock.
:::
