## What the hours do not see: labour intensity and platform work

The whole cognitive map counts labour in hours: an hour is an hour. There are two movements of contemporary capital that this measure does not register well, and both point in the same direction: they make the measurement in hours **underestimate** surplus labour. This section does not correct the figures; it reports that bias, its direction and what is known about its size.
{: .llano }

### The intensity of labour

Alongside the prolongation of the working day and the increase in productive force, Marx distinguishes a third path to surplus value: intensification, that is, more labour compressed into the same time, with fewer pores in the working day (Marx, 2018, ch. XIII). A more intensive hour creates more value than an hour of normal intensity, but in the accounts it appears the same. If intensity rises, the rate of surplus value in hours falls short of the effective one. No statistic measures intensity directly; since 1991 the European working conditions surveys have asked whether people work at high speed or to tight deadlines. What they show is a strong intensification between 1991 and 2005 and a high plateau since then: a third of European workers work at high speed three-quarters of the time or more.
{: .llano }

Sources: Eurofound (2007; 2016; 2025). The figures in each row are not comparable with those in other rows: the question, the threshold and the countries change. The 2021 telephone survey (49% frequently worked at high speed) used a different method and is not included.
{: .nota }

The cognitive map does not adjust hours by these indicators: they are workers' perceptions, and any conversion factor between ‘working at high speed’ and value produced would be arbitrary. Moreover, the data are European: there is no equivalent series for the United States or for the rest of the world. What can be asserted is the direction of the bias: where intensity rises or remains high, the measurement in hours does not exaggerate exploitation; if anything, it underestimates it.

### Platform work

Uber drivers, delivery riders and those who perform online tasks appear in the statistics as own-account (self-employed) workers. Under the cognitive map's general criterion, the income of own-account workers is not surplus value and they do not count as productive workers. But under the criterion of this research, platform work such as Uber's is productive labour in Marx's sense: the platform operates as a capitalist that subsumes the labour of the drivers. From the fare paid by the customer, it retains a commission—often between 20% and 40%—and hands the rest to the driver. That commission, after deducting the costs of servers, offices and software, is the form in which the surplus value extracted from living labour appears. The driver works one part of the working day to cover subsistence, petrol and the wear and tear of the car (necessary labour), and the rest for the platform (surplus labour). It is a form of piece wages (Marx, 2018, ch. XIX): it gives the appearance of greater freedom than a fixed wage and pushes workers themselves to lengthen and intensify their working day. The platform's capital—software, algorithms, brand—is valorised only when the driver logs on and makes the trip.
{: .llano }

Because the statistics record them as own-account workers, the cognitive map's measurement leaves these workers out: here too the bias is downwards. Their weight is still small in the United States, but it is growing worldwide.

| Indicator | Value | Source |
|---|---|---|
| US, work mediated by apps (May 2017) | 1.6 million people, 1.0% of employment | BLS (2018), Contingent Worker Supplement |
| US, independent contractors (July 2023) | 11.9 million, 7.4% of employment (includes, among others, platform workers) | BLS (2024a) |
| World, online platform workers (2023) | between 154 and 435 million, from 4.4% to 12.5% of the global labour force | World Bank (2023) |

The World Bank estimate covers only online work (remote tasks and services), not work done in a physical location, such as transport or delivery; the upper bound includes those who do it as a secondary activity. According to the same report, demand for online platform work grew by 41% between 2016 and the first quarter of 2023. The BLS has announced figures on app-based work for 2023, not yet published.
{: .nota }

:::como Where do these data come from and why do they not change the measurement?
- **Intensity.** Eurofound, European Working Conditions Survey (EWCS), waves of 1991, 1995, 2000, 2005, 2010, 2015 and 2024. The questions are: ‘Does your job involve working at very high speed?’ and ‘… working to tight deadlines?’, with a frequency scale; the percentages are of those answering ‘all of the time’, ‘almost all of the time’ or ‘around three-quarters of the time’, depending on the row. Coverage changes with the enlargement of the European Union.
- **Platforms.** BLS, Contingent Worker Supplement to the Current Population Survey (CPS), May 2017 (questions on electronically mediated work, recoded by the BLS) and July 2023. World Bank (2023), with two methods: workers registered on 545 platforms (154 million) and surveys (up to 435 million).
- **Why the figures are not adjusted.** Adjusting for intensity would require converting perception responses into hours of labour of normal intensity, and there is no basis for doing so. Adding platform workers would require their number, their hours and their net income by country and year, which do not exist as a series. Both cases remain as declared downward biases of the mass and of the rate of surplus value in hours.
:::
