## Explorer: one specification at a time

Choose an option at each fork and the chart draws the rate of profit that results from that combination of decisions, over the spread of the whole multiverse.

The fan above summarises hundreds of measurements; here each one can be looked at separately. Each menu is a theoretical decision: which sector is measured, what counts as profit or as surplus value, how capital is valued, whether intellectual property is included, and so on. When an option is changed, the explorer looks for the published specification that uses it and keeps as many of the other decisions as possible; if a decision makes no sense for that family of specifications (for example, the criterion of productive labour in the corporate series), its menu shows "not applicable". The coloured line is the chosen specification; the dotted lines, the years in which that specification shows a break in trend. Below are its level in three years and its identifier. What is worth comparing is the shape of the curve with that of the fan: where the theoretical decision changes the movement and where it only changes the level. The same information is in the [map](map.html) (by clicking a leaf or a fork) and, in full, in the [table of specifications](descargas/eeuu_multiverse_table_en.csv).

:::como How is this chart calculated?
Each specification is the annual rate r = numerator / denominator defined by its decisions (see the [methodology, §5](methodology.html#multiverso)). The chosen series is divided by its own period average and multiplied by 100, as in the fan; the within-industry specifications begin in 1972 and their average is taken from that year. The background fan (10th–90th and 25th–75th percentiles and median) is that of the 288 main specifications. The breaks are those the Bai–Perron procedure detects in that specification (methodology, §7). Levels are given in per cent, not normalised.

The downloadable table has one row per specification, with its decisions in words, the rate (%) in 1948, 1982 and the latest year, and the break years. This way of setting out every reasonable combination of decisions, rather than a single estimate, is what Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman and Vanpaemel (2016) call multiverse analysis.
:::
