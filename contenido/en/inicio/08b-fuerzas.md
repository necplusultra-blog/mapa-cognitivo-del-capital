## Productive forces and disposable time

The seismograph also records something that the profit figures conceal: the state of the productive forces that capital develops. Every increase in the productivity of labour reduces the time needed to reproduce those who produce. Under the capital relation, this freed time is not turned into free time but into surplus labour. What this section shows is not the working day of a future society, but the possibility that capital itself creates and, in the same movement, turns into labour for itself.

### United States: the time freed by productive force and what it is turned into (hours per week)

Three lines over the same 40-hour week of a productive worker in the United States. The first is actual necessary labour: the hours in which the worker produces the equivalent of their wage. The second is the time that would suffice, with each year's productivity, to produce the real wage that this worker earned in 1972: it falls much faster, because productivity grows faster than the wage. The third is surplus labour. The distance between the first and the second is what the development of productive force gave back to the workers as consumption; everything else remained as labour for capital. The normal working week was not shortened by a single hour.
{: .llano }

An additional line values the 1972 wage at the prices of what workers consume (the personal consumption expenditures price index, PCE) instead of the prices of the whole product. With that measure, {{fz-niv-pce}} per week would be needed for the 1972 standard of living, and the freed time is distributed as follows: {{fz-plus-pce-pct}} to surplus labour and {{fz-cons-pce-pct}} to more consumption. The actual week of those who work full-time, for its part, is not 40 hours: according to the US Current Population Survey, the usual weekly hours of full-time employees are {{fz-jtc-b}} in {{.fz-fin}} ({{fz-jtc0-b}} in {{fz-jtc-a0-b}}). With that week, weekly surplus labour goes from {{fz-jtc-plus0-b}} to {{fz-jtc-plus-b}}.

### World: daily minutes of productive labour per inhabitant

In the countries measured industry by industry ({{fz-wpct}} of the world population), the entire production of surplus value rests on little more than one hour a day of productive labour per inhabitant, and that time grows because capital incorporates more of the population into productive labour. Most of that time is surplus labour (with the gross rate and also with the rate net of depreciation); a smaller portion reproduces the productive workers themselves. The figure does not say how much each person would work in another society: it does not count the labour that is unproductive for capital but that every society needs (care, public health and education, administration), nor domestic labour, nor peasant labour. What it does show is how narrow the base of living labour is on which capital produces material wealth today. One line adds part of that labour which is unproductive for capital but socially necessary, that of employees in public and non-profit education and health ({{fz-wpub}} a day per inhabitant): with it, the total reaches {{fz-wsoc}}.
{: .llano }

:::como How are these data calculated?
**United States** (production and non-supervisory workers in the productive industries, within-industry variant, from 1972), for a full-time 40-hour week:

1. **Actual necessary labour** = 40 / (1 + e), with each year's rate of surplus value e (section ‘The United States in hours of labour’).
2. **Necessary time at the first year's standard of living** = 40 × w̃<sub>1972</sub> / μ̃<sub>t</sub>. w̃ is the real hourly compensation of productive workers (V / H); μ̃ is the real new value per hour of productive labour ((V + S) / H), the real monetary expression of labour time. Both are deflated by the GDP deflator, so that the ratio does not depend on the deflator chosen except for the difference between the prices of workers' consumption and those of the product.
2b. **At consumption prices (v2).** Time at the first year's standard of living = 40 × w<sub>1972</sub> × (PCE<sub>t</sub> / PCE<sub>1972</sub>) / μ<sub>t</sub>, in current dollars: hours that would suffice today to produce the value of the basket that the first year's wage bought (PCE: personal consumption expenditures price index, NIPA 1.1.4). The distribution of the freed time is recalculated with this measure.
2c. **Actual full-time week (v2).** Usual weekly hours of full-time employees in their main job (OECD, based on the Current Population Survey, CPS), from 1979; before that, the 1979 value is held constant. Necessary labour = week / (1 + e); surplus labour = week − necessary. These are usual hours, not hours actually worked in a given week.
3. **Destination of the freed time.** What productivity freed relative to the first year is the difference between that year's necessary labour and the time that would suffice today for the same standard of living. It is divided, exactly, into: more workers' consumption (actual necessary labour − time at the first year's level), more surplus labour (today's surplus labour − the first year's surplus labour) and reduction of the reference week (zero by construction, because the normal 40-hour week did not change).

**World** (countries measured industry by industry, central variant): annual hours of productive labour, of gross surplus labour and of necessary labour (productive hours − surplus labour), divided by the population of those same countries (Penn World Table; after its last year, average growth of the three preceding years) and by 365 days, in minutes. Surplus labour is gross: depreciation counts as part of the new value of living labour, unlike in the US section, where e is net. With the net e, necessary labour would be about 31 minutes a day instead of 24, and surplus labour about 43 instead of 50. Variant: the hours of employees in public and non-profit education and health are added (the difference between the variant with productive education and health and the central variant). Trade, finance and advertising, which are costs of circulation specific to the commodity form, are not added, nor is unpaid domestic and care work, which the accounts do not record.

These figures are not an estimate of the working day of an emancipated society: in such a society necessary labour would include the funds for replacing and expanding production and for common services, and it would be determined by the needs of the associated producers, not by the wage. They are a measure of how much time the development of productive force frees under capital and of what that time is turned into.
:::
