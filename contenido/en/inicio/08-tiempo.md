## From money to labour time: the United States

The mass of surplus value is the product of the rate of surplus value (pv/v, the degree of exploitation) and the number of productively exploited workers. Measured in money, the rate of surplus value of productive workers expresses the proportion between surplus labour and necessary labour in their working day: of each week of labour, a fraction 1/(1+e) reproduces the value of their labour power and a fraction e/(1+e) is surplus labour.

Everything in this section refers **only to the United States** and to production and non-supervisory workers employed by capital in the productive industries (excluding government and non-profit institutions), from 1972. The world scale in hours is further down, in its own section.

### United States: the productive worker's week, divided into necessary labour and surplus labour (hours)

A full-time 40-hour week of a productive worker in the United States, split in two according to each year's rate of surplus value. One line is the hours in which the worker produces the equivalent of their wage (necessary labour); the other, the hours in which they work for capital without receiving anything in return (surplus labour). Necessary labour falls from 13.8 to 9.7 hours since 1972, at an uneven pace: it is compressed sharply between 2000 and 2007 and almost stalls afterwards. That it cannot reach zero is a property of the form of the working day, not an explanation of that pause, which has historical causes. The third line is the average paid week of all production workers, full-time and part-time: it is not a working day, and it falls mainly because industries with short working hours, such as hospitality, are growing.
{: .llano }

Each year, the 40-hour week is divided according to that same year's rate of surplus value: necessary hours = 40 / (1 + e); surplus labour = 40 × e / (1 + e). The average paid week comes from the BLS establishment survey (CES).
{: .nota }

### United States: annual mass of surplus labour (billion hours)

It adds up the hours of surplus labour of all productive workers in the United States in a year. It has more than doubled since 1972, and almost all of that growth comes from there being more workers, not from each one working more hours for capital. The falls coincide with recessions, when millions are laid off.
{: .llano }

Hours worked in the year by all productive workers in the United States × e / (1 + e). It is the sum of unpaid hours, not an average.

:::como+ How are these data obtained?
1. **Productive workers (N).** Sum of the employment of production and non-supervisory workers (BLS, CES survey, series 06) in the productive supersectors: mining and logging, construction, manufacturing, transportation and warehousing, utilities, information, professional and business services, private education and health, leisure and hospitality, other services. Excluded are trade, finance, government, supervisors and agriculture (which the CES does not cover). From three supersectors the part that is not productive capital is subtracted: from professional services, legal and accounting services (circulation industries); from private education and health, the part employed by non-profit institutions (70% of education, 43% of health and 40% of social assistance, according to the BLS for 2022); from other services, the non-profit part of religious and civic organisations (60%). Before 1990, lacking industry detail, the 1990 proportions are used. Annual average, in millions.
2. **Hours (H).** For each supersector, employment × average weekly hours paid (series 07; in manufacturing, AWHMAN) × 52.
3. **Rate of surplus value (e).** S/V from the previous section, in its within-industry variant: S is the net broad surplus value of the business sector and V the compensation of those same workers (employment × hourly wage × hours × 52, scaled up to include benefits).
4. **Working day.** For a full-time 40-hour week: necessary labour = 40 / (1 + e); surplus labour = 40 × e / (1 + e). The average paid week is H / N / 52: it mixes full-time and part-time work, and these are paid hours (it includes paid holidays and excludes unpaid overtime). This gives the first chart.
5. **Mass of surplus labour.** H × e / (1 + e) = N × hours per worker × e / (1 + e), in billions of hours per year. This gives the second chart.
6. **Decomposition.** Between the first and the last year, each factor contributes ln(factor<sub>end</sub>/factor<sub>start</sub>) / ln(mass<sub>end</sub>/mass<sub>start</sub>); the three contributions add up to 100%.

The key assumption of the macro-monetary interpretation (Moseley, 2016) is that v is the wage actually paid, so that the monetary proportion pv/v is also the proportion between surplus labour and necessary labour.
:::

### Where does the growth in the mass of surplus labour come from?

The table separates that growth into three causes: more workers, more or fewer paid hours per worker (which fall mainly because part-time work and industries with short working hours are growing, not because the working day is being shortened) and a larger proportion of surplus labour within the working day.

### Monetary expression of labour time in the United States

How many dollars of new value one hour of productive labour represents, on average. It is the bridge that allows figures in money to be read as figures in labour time. In current dollars it rises with inflation; in 2017 dollars it rises much less.
{: .llano }

New value (v + pv) per hour of productive labour: how many dollars one hour of productive social labour represents. It is the relation that makes it possible to pass from money to time and from time to money.
{: .nota }
