## Collapse? Kurz’s theory and the data

Robert Kurz’s theory of collapse is the most consistent formulation of the idea that capital is heading towards an absolute internal limit. Its core can be summarised in three claims. First: the production of relative surplus value and the scientisation of production expel living labour from the immediate process of production, and this expulsion, beyond a certain threshold of the productive force, ceases to be relative and becomes absolute: the number of workers productive for capital diminishes. Second: with them the global mass of surplus value diminishes, because abstract labour, understood as the expenditure of human energy, is the substance of value. Third: the microelectronic revolution, and all the more so the technologies that extend it, only accelerate this process; credit and fictitious capital postpone it without reversing it (Kurz, 2012, pp. 300–312; see also Kurz, 2021).

All three are claims about magnitudes, and the seismograph measures those magnitudes. That a theory cannot be refuted with statistics, because its categories are not those of the national accounts, does not exempt it from confronting the phenomenal manifestation of what it claims. What follows neither demonstrates nor refutes categories; it shows what form the movement the theory describes has taken in the available data.

### Has the surplus-value-producing proletariat diminished?

No, neither at the centre nor on the world scale. In the United States, production and non-supervisory workers employed by productive capital go from 33.5 million in 1972 to 70.5 million in 2025 (not counting state employees or those of non-profit institutions). They grow not only in number: they grow as a proportion of civilian employment, from 41% to 43%. In the 42 countries measured industry by industry, productive wage earners go from 767 million in 2000 to 1,077 million in 2025. Of those 42 countries, only two (Russia and Romania) have fewer productive workers today than in 2000.

The expulsion that Kurz describes exists, but it has a precise location. In US manufacturing, production workers fall from 14.5 million in 1979 to 8.1 million in 2010, and barely recover afterwards (8.8 million in 2025). That absolute expulsion in industry is real. What is not borne out is its generalisation: over the same period, capital opened up or capitalised entire industries —transport and logistics, business services, for-profit health and education, leisure and hospitality— that absorbed more productive workers than manufacturing expelled. And outside the centre, the expanded reproduction of world capital incorporated hundreds of millions of people in China, India and the rest of Asia into capitalist production. If productive labour is identified with industrial labour, as the image of collapse tends to do, one of the forms of expulsion is taken for its global result.

### Is the global mass of surplus value diminishing?

Nor that. In the United States, the mass of surplus value of the business sector, in purchasing power, increases more than eightfold between 1948 and 2025, and the annual mass of surplus labour of productive workers goes from 42.5 billion hours in 1972 to 94.1 billion in 2025. On the world scale, the gross mass of surplus labour grows from 1,077 to 1,476 billion hours between 2000 and 2025.

There is, however, one datum that must be recorded with the same honesty. If the consumption of fixed capital is subtracted from surplus value, the net world mass of surplus labour grows until 2018 (1,261 billion hours) and has stagnated since then (1,265 billion in 2025). This is not a decline, and the estimate of depreciation for China and India is the most uncertain part of the whole measurement. Nor is it the first: after the 2008 crisis the net mass fell for three consecutive years and did not recover its 2007 level until 2012. But the current one is the longest stagnation of the quarter-century measured. This stagnation does not confirm an exhaustion of the substance of value; it does show that replacing the mass requires a growing portion of new labour, and that this requirement may come to absorb the growth.

### Do the new technologies accelerate expulsion?

The period of the platform economy and of massive investment in artificial intelligence is still short, but it shows no acceleration of expulsion. Between 2018 and 2025, US productive workers increase by 6% and their mass of surplus labour by 7%. What the measurement does show is another movement: a handful of corporations that control computing, chips and models appropriate almost a fifth of US corporate profits. The new technology appears, for now, less as a mechanism of general expulsion than as a mechanism of appropriation: a monopolised general condition of production, through which surplus value produced in other industries is redistributed.

### Does increased exploitation compensate for the expulsion of workers?

The *Capital & Class* article (Jiménez Cea, 2026) argues that the expulsion of living labour can be compensated, within certain limits, by an increase in the degree of exploitation. This is Marx’s thesis in Volume III: the compensation for the decrease in the number of workers by the increase in the degree of exploitation runs up against insuperable limits, and for that reason it can check the fall in the rate ‘but not cancel it’ (Marx, 2021, p. 318). The data allow us to look at it in three ways.

**First, where productive workers diminish.** Between 2000 and 2014 there are nine countries, out of the 42, in which the number of productive wage earners falls. In five of them the rate of surplus value rises enough to compensate wholly or almost wholly for the fall in the mass: Slovakia, Slovenia and the Netherlands end the period with more surplus labour than at the start, Romania loses 1% of its mass with 10% fewer workers, and Portugal 9% with 14% fewer. In the other four (Russia, Greece, Estonia and Denmark) the rate of surplus value also falls and the mass falls more than the number of workers. In the United States, in the years when productive employment fell, the compensation is partial and uneven: the mass falls less than the number of workers in some years (1975, 2002–2003, 2010, 2020; in 2020, mainly because of average hours), and not in others (1980, 1982, 1991, 2001, 2008, 2009). The compensation exists, but it is not automatic: it depends on capital managing to impose a higher rate of exploitation, and that is a result of struggle and of crisis, not a mechanical law.

**Second, the limit of compensation.** In the working day of the US productive worker, surplus labour today occupies 76% of the time (30.3 of 40 hours). Even if necessary labour could be reduced to almost nothing, surplus labour per worker could grow by only 32% with that working day. In other words: with the given working day, no increase in exploitation could compensate for a fall of more than 24% in the number of productive workers. In 1972 that margin was 35%. (The margin is the necessary part of the working day, 1/(1+e): it does not depend on how many hours the working day has.) This is not a distance to the limit of capital —the working day can be lengthened and intensified, and necessary labour never reaches zero— but the arithmetical form that Marx’s example takes in the given working day: two workers working twelve hours do not yield the surplus labour of twenty-four working two (Marx, 2018, pp. 372–373). That margin narrows as exploitation increases: it is an arithmetical property of the form of the working day, not a measure of how much capital has left.

**Third, what has actually sustained the mass.** Compensation through exploitation has not, so far, been the main mechanism. In the United States, 94% of the growth in the mass of surplus labour between 1972 and 2025 is explained by the number of workers, and only 19% by the increase in the share of surplus labour in the working day (the reduction in average paid hours subtracts 12%). On the world scale, the rate of surplus value in hours does not rise (2.05 in 2000, 2.07 in 2025): the mass grows through proletarianisation. Where compensation through exploitation does prove decisive is in the *rate* of profit: between 1975 and 2004, the increase in the rate of surplus value counteracts the rising composition; between 2004 and 2016, when it stops rising, the rate of profit falls in all specifications.

### What remains of the theory of collapse

The image of a general and absolute expulsion of the productive proletariat, and of a diminishing mass of surplus value, finds no support in the available data. But the truth content of Kurz’s theory does not disappear on that account. The three paths by which capital has sustained its mass —incorporating new populations, opening up new industries, raising exploitation— each have their own limit: the exploitable population is not unlimited, the opening up of industries requires ever more capital per worker, and exploitation grows asymptotically. The net mass stagnating since 2018 and the fall in the rate between 2004 and 2016 are the manifestations of that growing requirement.

The difference from the theory of collapse lies not in denying the limits, but in their form. What the measurement shows is not a linear and irreversible exhaustion of the substance of value, but a social relation that reconstitutes itself again and again, on a broader basis and with greater violence, while undermining its own conditions. This reconstitution is not a destiny: it is the result of a contradictory social praxis, in which the same forces that capital mobilises to sustain itself are also those that could put an end to it.
