## What can be asked of a measurement

National accounts do not record surplus value, variable capital or productive labour. They record prices, wages, jobs and stocks valued in money. Translating them into categories of the critique of political economy requires theoretical decisions, and that is why the cognitive map does not offer *a* single measurement of the rate of profit, but a specification space — the set of possible calculations — in which each fork corresponds to an explicit theoretical decision. What withstands all the different theoretical decisions can be read as a manifestation of the dynamics of capital; what changes with them shows where theory decides the result and the way in which that tendency is read.

To clarify, and to be more explicit about the project's methodology, this means that there is no single correct way of measuring the rate of profit, so the project does not give a single measurement parameter. Indeed, any attempt to measure the rate of profit requires several theoretical decisions before calculating. For example:

- which workers count as productive;
- whether capital is valued at what it cost or at what it would cost today;
- whether depreciation is deducted;
- whether, for example, software, research and intellectual property count as capital.

Each possible answer to each question leads down a different path, and that is a 'fork'. So, given the enormous quantity of data at my disposal, instead of choosing one path and hiding the others, I arranged for the project to compute the rate along all the paths at once, using artificial intelligence.

'Specification', for its part, is the technical term for a complete and precise way of calculating something. A specification of the rate of profit is defined once everything has been fixed:

- what goes into the numerator and the denominator;
- with which data it is calculated;
- which kinds of labour count as producing surplus value;
- how capital is valued.

If a single one of those decisions changes, the result is another specification. Combining these decisions yields 288 different versions of the calculation of the rate of profit, and that is the set of possible calculations of the rate of profit. Each version states which decisions it made, so that no one auditing this project has to guess where each number comes from.

The idea comes from social-science methodology. Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman and Vanpaemel proposed it in 2016 under the name *multiverse analysis*; Simonsohn, Simmons and Nelson (2020) formalised it as *specification curve analysis*. The core of their argument is that when a study presents a single result, it hides all the other reasonable decisions it could have made, and with them all the possible results. Each combination of theoretical decisions in a study produces a possible result, so showing them all together at the same time lets the rest of the research community see clearly which conclusions hold across all the specifications and which depend on a particular choice.

In this project of cognitive mapping of contemporary capitalism and its fundamental tendencies, then, 'specification space' means the set of 288 ways of calculating, in the first place, the US rate of profit that result from combining the theoretical decisions disputed among the Marxist currents. None is presented as the true one. What matters is what they all show together: for example, the post-war fall to the mid-1970s, which appears in almost all of them. I chose that country because, although I would have liked to complement it with China, I unfortunately did not have as much data on China as on the United States, since much of it is not published by the country.

Finally, the caution that led me to choose this method has a deeper ground than statistical prudence. If the immanent limits of capital were some kind of reachable ceiling, a well-made measurement could say how close the system is to reaching it. But the limit that the capital relation cannot cross —the reduction to zero of the labour necessary for the reproduction of the working class— has the form of an asymptote: capital approaches it without being able to reach it, because reaching it would abolish the relation itself (Marx, 2018, p. 741; 2016a, pp. 282–284). No statistical series, then, measures a 'distance' to the limit. What can be observed is the form in which capital processes its limits today: with what rates of exploitation, what masses of labour, what composition of capital and what countertendencies. The series show the phenomenal manifestation of those determinations; they do not demonstrate them by themselves.
