## Six readings of the same data

The currents in the Marxist debate on the rate of profit are not distinguished only by their definitions. Each isolates one moment of the movement that the series record and turns it into the centre of its diagnosis. Having them read the same data —the [chart and cards on the main page](./#referencias)— makes it possible to see what each one captures and what it leaves out. They are grouped here by the moment they privilege.

### The tendency: Kliman, Roberts and Carchedi

Kliman (2012), Roberts (2016) and Carchedi and Roberts (2018; 2022) read the series as confirmation of a long-term fall. The data bear them out on one decisive point. In the broad measures of surplus value, the US rate in 2025 is still below that of 1948: by 27% with fixed capital, by 22% with total capital advanced. The net world rate from the Penn World Table falls from 8.5% in 2007 to 5.2% in 2023. And between 2004 and 2016 the rate falls in all the business specifications of the multiverse. Recomputed with each author’s definition and current data ([the recomputed series](./#replicacion)), the changes Roberts (2018) published are reproduced almost exactly: −20% between 1965 and 1982 and +8% between 1982 and 1997, at historical cost. And measured by property income or by total profit over capital at historical cost, as Kliman measures it, the corporate rate does not recover between 1982 and 1997 (−13% and −15%).

But the same data contradict the form these readings tend to give the tendency. Between 1982 and 2000 the composition of capital falls and the rate rises in the current-cost measures (not in Kliman’s, at historical cost, which falls). The real mass of US surplus value increases more than eightfold since 1948, and the world mass of surplus labour grows by 37% between 2000 and 2025. The historical-cost rate that Kliman privileges goes from 16.8% in 2019 to 23.4% in 2025, largely because of inflation, which inflates current profit relative to a capital valued at past prices. The no-recovery thesis, moreover, depends on the measure: over the same period, pre-tax profit over capital at historical cost rises by 8%. And a good part of the fall in the world rate occurs in China, where accumulation is greatest. A ‘long depression’ in which the mass of exploited surplus labour keeps growing is a characterisation that is hard to sustain. The fall in the rate is not the halting of accumulation but the form accumulation takes when it accelerates.

### The countertendency: Duménil and Lévy, Heinrich

Duménil and Lévy (2004; 2011) and Heinrich (2013; 2014) place the emphasis on the recovery and on the indeterminacy of the movement. These readings, too, capture something the series show unambiguously. The rate recovers after 1982 in the measures of surplus value and of business profit (in those of the Duménil–Lévy and Kliman type, only after 2001), and that recovery coincides with a redistribution against workers: the within-industry rate of surplus value rises from 2.16 in 1982 to 3.14 in 2025. Heinrich is right that the composition does not always rise: the cheapening of the elements of constant capital made it fall for two decades. And the spread of the multiverse shows how much of the level and direction of the rate depends on the theory adopted.

The replication, however, introduces a correction to this reading. With today’s data, the recovery of the broad corporate rate between 1982 and 2000, which Duménil and Lévy (2002) measured at 45%, is 13% (17% without intellectual property). The neoliberal restoration of profitability was, up to 2000, weaker than the accounts of the time showed; the corporate rate rises mainly afterwards.

But if the movement were indeterminate, or purely political, nothing should withstand all the specifications. What does withstand them is significant. When the rate of surplus value stopped rising, between 2004 and 2016, the rate of profit fell in all of them, under both criteria of productive labour. The neoliberal recovery was not a self-sustaining order but a countertendency that runs up against the form of the working day: the rate of surplus value can keep rising but, in that form, each increase compresses necessary labour less (a determination of the form, not a tendency the series show decade by decade).

### Distribution: Shaikh and Moseley

The readings of Shaikh and Tonak (1994), Shaikh (2016) and Moseley (1991) make visible the difference between surplus value produced and profit appropriated, and the data confirm it. Since the 1982 trough, non-financial corporate profit recovers more (+47%) than broad surplus value by industry (+22%). The within-industry rate of profit exceeds its 1974–1979 levels (not those of 1972–1973), and the within-industry rate of surplus value exceeds those of the whole decade, while the corporate rate does not return to its 1960s levels. That difference is the space occupied by unproductive labour, interest and rent. The replication confirms it: Shaikh and Tonak’s rate of surplus value, recomputed with the wages of production workers, reproduces the one they published for 1975–1982 and, without deducting what households pay to finance, keeps rising to 3.28 in 2025. The ratio of unproductive to productive labour that Moseley (1997) gives for 1977 (0.94) is the same with today’s data, and rises to 1.54 in 2025. The scope of these readings has a limit: distribution does not create surplus value. The relative slowdown of unproductive expenditure and the fall in interest enlarge the appropriated share of a mass whose growth depends on something else.

## A critical reading: two sides of one and the same movement

The six readings isolate moments of what, in Marx’s exposition, is a single contradictory movement. The law of the tendential fall of the rate of profit is two-sided (Marx, 2021, pp. 277–280; Jiménez Cea, 2026): the same laws of accumulation produce a rate subject to downward pressure and a growing absolute mass of surplus value. The countertendencies are not external factors that correct the tendency from outside, but the form in which the tendency is realised. Read in this way, the series cease to be divided between a ‘tendency’ confirmed by some and a ‘recovery’ confirmed by others. What they show is the way in which capital processes its immanent limits.

**Neither collapse nor unlimited valorisation.** The mass of surplus value grows in the United States and in the world, and the rate does not recover its post-war level in the broad measures. The readings of the tendency see only the first half of this sentence; those of the countertendency, only the second.

**Exploitation grows asymptotically.** At the centre, the decisive countertendency is the raising of the rate of surplus value. In a 40-hour week, the necessary labour of the US productive worker goes from 13.8 hours in 1972 to 9.7 in 2025. In the form of the working day, each hour subtracted from necessary labour increases surplus labour in a smaller proportion than the previous one; the series do not show diminishing returns decade by decade (the compression of 2000–2007 was faster than that of 1982–2000). Heinrich is right that the rate of surplus value has no finite maximum. What does have a limit is the surplus labour that fits into a working day, because necessary labour cannot reach zero without abolishing the capital relation. That the fall in the rate of profit cannot be deduced in general does not make indeterminate the form in which capital strains against that limit: the 2004–2016 phase shows what happens when that countertendency comes to a halt.

**The mass is sustained by simultaneous working days.** 94% of the growth in the US mass of surplus labour since 1972 is explained by the increase in the number of productive workers. On the world scale, the rate of surplus value in hours does not rise, and the mass grows through proletarianisation, above all in China and India. This is the other limit of the mass of surplus value: the number of working days that capital manages to set to work at the same time. Reabsorbing population requires ever more capital per worker, and the world mass net of depreciation has stagnated since 2018.

**Distribution transfers and anticipates surplus value.** The difference between surplus value produced and profit appropriated that Shaikh, Moseley and Duménil and Lévy see is the terrain of a third mode of the countertendencies. Foreign trade transfers hours of labour towards high-income countries. Households pay to finance, out of their wages, a growing portion of their income: surplus value displaced from the wage, not produced, which the seismograph no longer counts as surplus value. A handful of artificial intelligence corporations intercept almost a fifth of US corporate profits. And the equities of non-financial corporations go from 35% to 216% of GDP: claims on surplus labour that has not yet been produced. None of these movements by itself increases the global mass of surplus value.

What these four observations allow us to determine is not how close capital is to a ceiling. That question is shared —even if they answer it in opposite ways— by the readings of exhaustion and those of indeterminacy. What can be determined is how the entire field of countertendencies is articulated today: with an exploitation that strains against a working day whose form limits what each compression yields, a mass that depends on reabsorbing population with more capital per worker, and an appropriation that grows faster than production. The hypothesis of borderline capitalism is a hypothesis about that articulation. This site does not demonstrate it: it shows its phenomenal manifestation and submits it to testing.
