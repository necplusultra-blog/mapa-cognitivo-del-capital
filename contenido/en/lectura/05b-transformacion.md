## Values, prices and the world market

The formation of prices neither creates nor destroys surplus value: it redistributes it. The input–output tables of 43 countries make it possible to see this industry by industry between 2000 and 2014. Market prices deviate from values by 28% on average, and the direction of the deviation is the one Marx anticipated (Marx, 2021, Part Two): in almost all countries, industries with more capital per unit of labour sell above their value and those with more living labour, below it. Agriculture, fishing, textiles and hotels and restaurants cede value; petroleum refining, mining, energy, metals and chemicals appropriate it. The equalisation of profit rates is a mechanism of transfer from where more living labour is exploited to where more capital is accumulated. With Moseley’s (2016) interpretation, in which money costs are the same for values and prices, competition shifts around 29% of each country’s surplus value between industries (2014 median), and the effective rates by industry end up closer to one another than if each industry realised its own surplus value: equalisation exists, but it is partial. The temporal variant (TSSI) does not change the picture; it only raises the rate in years of inflation. The two agree on what matters for this reading: the rate of profit of the productive industries of the 43 countries rises until 2007 and falls thereafter (from 26.8% to 23.2% in 2014 with Moseley). Since version 3, education and health enter through their capitalist part, and they turn out to be among the industries that cede the most surplus value: their capital per worker is low.

The same logic operates between countries. Counting the labour contained in inputs, China and India each hand over, in 2014, more than 100 billion hours of labour more than they receive in their trade, and the high-income countries receive some 300 billion within the group of 43 countries. The OECD’s world tables, which value each imported input with the hours of the country that produced it, show the same pattern between 2000 and 2024, with a shift: the high-income countries receive between 230 and 390 billion hours a year; China, which handed over more than 200 billion in the mid-2000s, has handed over fewer than 80 since 2020, while India goes from 78 to more than 110. The transfer of labour through trade shifts with the relocation of production. And on the world market an hour of national labour is not worth the same everywhere, because the more productive national labour counts as more intensive labour (Marx, 2018, ch. XX): if each hour is weighted by the monetary productivity of its country, China’s share of the world mass of surplus labour falls from 33% to 18%, though it grows rapidly.

This sharpens the reading of the world mass. Measured in hours of exploited labour, the mass rests above all on China and India; measured in world-market hours, the centre retains a much greater weight, because its hours are worth more. The two measures are true at the same time: world capital reabsorbs population in the periphery, and the surplus value produced there is realised and redistributed, to a large extent, on the centre’s terms. The measurement is compatible with a theoretical reading that it does not demonstrate: that the limit capital faces is not the scarcity of exploitable living labour, but the growing requirement of capital and of appropriation to convert it into value that counts on the world market.
