## The transformation: values and prices

**Aggregate level.** The cognitive map works at the macro-monetary level (Moseley, 2016; close to the ‘new interpretation’ of Foley, 1982, and Duménil, 1983): total surplus value is, by construction, the sum of profits, interest, rents, taxes on production and unproductive salaries, and the monetary expression of labour time translates money into hours. At that level, the transformation problem does not alter the mass or the rate of surplus value. What it does alter is the distribution of surplus value among industries and countries, which is what this phase measures.

**Source.** WIOD national input-output tables (November 2016 release), 43 countries, 56 industries, 2000–2014, in millions of dollars, with the domestic input matrix and the imported input matrix; hours and persons employed from the WIOD Socio-Economic Accounts.

**Values.** For the productive industries p:

<div class="f">λ<sub>p</sub> = (l<sub>p</sub> + λ̄<sub>p</sub> M<sub>pp</sub>) (I − A<sub>pp</sub>)<sup>−1</sup></div>

l<sub>j</sub> = hours worked by all persons employed in the industry / gross output. The hours of non-employees are approximated by the hours per employee of the same industry (H\_EMPE / EMPE × EMP); in China, 46 h × 50 weeks per person employed. Own-account workers count for value (they produce commodities) though not for surplus value. λ̄<sub>j</sub> = Σ<sub>c</sub> λ<sub>cj</sub> X<sub>cj</sub> / Σ<sub>c</sub> X<sub>cj</sub>: average hours content of one exported dollar of product j. It is solved by iteration until λ̄ no longer changes.

The circulation industries (G, K, M69–M70, M73) and those outside capitalist production (L, O, T, U) do not produce value, and their products used as inputs do not transfer value: they are expenses paid out of surplus value (Shaikh and Tonak, 1994). Education (P) and health (Q) enter through their capitalist part, φ = 1 − g, with g the public and non-profit share of employment that is also used in the world mass: λ = (l + (λ̄ ∘ φ) · M) · (I − D<sub>φ</sub> A)<sup>−1</sup>, and in the Moseley measurement all their magnitudes are multiplied by φ. The capitalist part is assumed to have the same cost structure as the whole industry.

**Prices and values.** μ = Σ<sub>p</sub> x<sub>j</sub> / Σ<sub>p</sub> λ<sub>j</sub> x<sub>j</sub> (dollars per hour); price-value<sub>j</sub> = μ λ<sub>j</sub>; mean deviation = Σ x<sub>j</sub> |μλ<sub>j</sub> − 1| / Σ x<sub>j</sub>. The Spearman rank correlation between price/value and the composition K/v (nominal capital stock over the industry's labour income, LAB) tests Marx's expectation: prices of production exceed values where the composition is high.

**Foreign trade.** Hours delivered = Σ λ<sub>j</sub> X<sub>j</sub>; hours received = Σ λ̄<sub>j</sub> M<sub>j</sub> × (X / M), with X and M the exports and imports of products of productive industries (imports include inputs and final demand).

**World-market weighting.** w<sub>c</sub> = μ<sub>c</sub> / μ̄, with μ<sub>c</sub> = GDP in current dollars / hours worked (World Bank, PWT) and μ̄ the average, weighted by productive hours, of the 42 countries in the world mass. Weighted mass = Σ surplus labour<sub>c</sub> w<sub>c</sub>. Market exchange rates are used, not purchasing power parities, because what is at issue is valuation on the world market.

**Interpretation of the transformation (F4 v2; Pablo's decision, 24 Nov. 2026).** The central interpretation is Moseley's (2016): constant and variable capital are taken as the money magnitudes actually advanced, identical in the determination of values and of prices of production, so that the transformation only redistributes total surplus value among industries. Value of industry j: W<sub>j</sub> = c<sub>j</sub> + a<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub>(1 + e); price of production: PP<sub>j</sub> = c<sub>j</sub> + a<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub> + r(K<sub>j</sub> + v<sub>j</sub>), with e = S/V and r = S/(K + V) of the country, and a<sub>j</sub> the labour income of non-employees. As a contrast, the temporal single-system variant is calculated (TSSI; Freeman and Carchedi, 1996; Kliman, 2007): inputs are valued at the prices at which they entered production (the industry's input price index for the previous year) and the capital advanced is the stock at the close of the previous year; new value is output minus those inputs. Results: `transformacion_moseley_tssi.csv` (by country and year) and `transformacion_moseley_ramas.csv` (by industry).

**Reconciliation of the two measurements (v3).** Agriculture and the industries with many own-account workers appeared to be ceding value in the measurement in hours and gaining in Moseley's. The two measurements answer different questions (market price versus value; price of production versus value), and they also value own-account labour differently: by its hours or by its money income. To separate this, value/price in hours is calculated counting only the hours of employees (l<sub>j</sub> × employees<sub>j</sub> / persons employed<sub>j</sub>, WIOD SEA) and compared with Moseley's W<sub>j</sub> / output<sub>j</sub>. In agriculture, that valuation explains about three-quarters of the gap (median of countries and years); the rest is due to the fact that in hours every hour counts the same, whereas in Moseley's measurement the wage paid is what counts. Counting only employees' hours, agriculture sells close to its value. China, which WIOD records without separating employees (COMP = LAB), enters with the same assumption as in the world mass: ILO employees distributed across non-agricultural industries and none in family agriculture; v = compensation × s and a = LAB − v (correction from the November 2026 audit; previously China entered as if all its persons employed were employees, which inverted Moseley's agricultural value/price).

**OECD world tables (v3: 2000–2024).** `herramientas/icio.py` calculates values with the ICIO world matrix (regular version), 2026 edition, for every year (84 economies, twelve rest-of-world regions and 50 industries; 2023 and 2024 are OECD projections). Employment by industry is the OECD's (TiM 2025, up to 2022; afterwards, the 2022 structure scaled with PWT total employment) and hours per person employed are the PWT's: λ = l · (I − D<sub>φ</sub> A)<sup>−1</sup>, with the country of origin of each input. In professional services (M), φ is the share of architecture and engineering, research and other professional services in the output of M according to WIOD 2014. For regions and countries without employment in TiM, l is imputed with the world median of hours per dollar of value added of the industry; they enter the matrix, but not the medians, which cover the 68 economies with measured employment. Results: mean deviation of prices from values between 34% and 40% (median); in 2024, DEU 21%, USA 35%, CHN 49%, IND 80%. Trade: high-income countries receive 348 billion hours in 2000, 388 in 2005 (the peak), 299 in 2014 and 271 in 2024; China delivers 181 (2000), 231 (2005), 141 (2014) and 78 (2024); India, 77 (2000), 137 (2013) and 113 (2024). The transfers of all countries do not sum exactly to zero (−17.7 billion hours in 2024), because hours received are rescaled by each country's exports/imports ratio and the imputed regions are not counted. ‘High income’ follows the World Bank classification, which does not include Taiwan; with Taiwan, the 2024 figure would be 276. With WIOD, China delivered 146 in 2014, against 141 with the OECD in the same year: the two sources almost entirely coincide, even though the OECD values the labour contained in imports with the hours of the countries of origin and WIOD with the world average. For 2000–2005, the 2025 edition gave almost identical results.

**Pending decisions and limits.** The values in hours in the first part are simultaneous (values and prices of the same year). The national tables do not say which country imported inputs come from; they are valued with the world average for each product. WIOD ends in 2014; the OECD world tables cover 2000–2024.
