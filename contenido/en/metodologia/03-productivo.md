## Productive and unproductive labour

**Criterion.** Productive labour is labour that is exchanged for money as capital in the sphere of production and produces surplus value. The concrete content of the activity is irrelevant, so the labour of capitalist enterprises in education, health or software is productive. There are two kinds of unproductive labour:

- labour exchanged for capital in circulation, whose wage is a deduction from the total mass of surplus value (*faux frais*);
- labour exchanged for revenue: the state, personal services, non-profit institutions. Labour paid out of the part of surplus value that the state appropriates through taxes is unproductive, even if it produces use values.

By contrast, wage labour employed by state enterprises that sell commodities for profit is productive: what makes a worker productive is that their wage is money exchanged as capital, and it makes no difference whether it is paid by a private capitalist or by a state enterprise run by a bureaucracy. In both cases capital is accumulated (author's decision).

Labour that produces software, R&D and other intellectual property products in capitalist enterprises is productive.

**Platform work (author's decision, 25 Nov. 2026).** The labour of drivers and couriers for platforms such as Uber is productive in Marx's sense, even though the statistics record it as own-account work. The platform operates as a capitalist that subsumes this labour: it retains a commission on the fare (often between 20% and 40%) which, after deducting its costs for servers, offices and software, is the phenomenal form of surplus value; the driver works one part of the working day to replace their subsistence, the fuel and the wear and tear of the vehicle (necessary labour) and the rest for the platform (surplus labour). It is a form of piece wages (Marx, 2018, ch. XIX), and the platform's capital is valorised only when the worker logs on. Since there are no series on their number, hours and earnings by country, the measurement in hours does not yet include them: this is a declared downward bias (home page, ‘What the hours do not see’).

**Intellectual property: a dual character that cannot be settled in the abstract (author's decision, 24 Nov. 2026).** Two things converge in intellectual property products. The development and continuous updating of a program — new features, new versions — is the labour of employees of capitalist corporations, which produces surplus value according to the same laws as all commodity production. But once created, the program is reproduced at almost zero cost, and restricting its use through licences means charging a rent on a product that is practically infinitely reproducible. Which of the two determinations predominates depends on each case, and the growing share of software produced with the help of artificial intelligence — which multiplies programmers' productivity — makes the boundary even more mobile. That is why the site does not fix a single treatment: the inclusion of intellectual property in K (as fixed capital that transfers value) or its exclusion (as a title to a rent) is a fork of the specification space (§5). The reference measures include it, as the national accounts do; the spread of the specifications shows how much the result changes without it.

**By industry (variant b).** Wholesale and retail trade, finance and insurance, real estate and rental and leasing, and legal services are unproductive. The rest of the business sector is productive.

**Within-industry correction (variant c, from 1972).** Within the productive industries, only production and non-supervisory workers, as defined by the BLS, count as variable capital. For each productive supersector:

<div class="f">wage bill = employment × hourly earnings × weekly hours × 52</div>

The sum of these wage bills is scaled by the business sector's compensation/wages ratio, to include benefits. Manufacturing uses the hours series `AWHMAN`. Agriculture is left out, because the CES does not cover it.

Three supersectors are adjusted to bring the within-industry criterion closer to the by-industry criterion and to the numerator, which is the value added of the business sector (excluding government and non-profit institutions): from professional and business services, legal (5411) and accounting (5412) services are subtracted; from private education and health, the share employed by non-profit institutions (70% of employment in education, 43% in health, 40% in social assistance, calculated on the total employment of each supersector; 2022 data from BLS, 2024b, and Newhouse and Abramson, 2024); from other services, the non-profit share of religious, civic and similar organisations (60%). The non-profit shares are held constant (the BLS indicates that they barely change between 2007 and 2022); before 1990 the 1990 structure is used. Advertising (5418) has no monthly employment series and stays in. Limitation: the total employment of each industry is used as a proxy for the employment of production workers. The two criteria do not fully coincide: the within-industry adjustment also subtracts accounting services, which the by-industry criterion cannot separate from the rest of professional services, and it does not cover agriculture, which is productive under the by-industry criterion. That is why the difference between V<sub>b</sub> and V<sub>c</sub> that the main page shows as ‘supervision’ also includes agriculture (about 1% of V<sub>b</sub>), accountants and differences in coverage.

Productive supersectors: mining and logging, construction, manufacturing, transportation and warehousing, electricity, gas and water, information, professional and business services, private education and health, leisure and hospitality, other services. Unproductive: wholesale trade, retail trade, financial activities.

**Variant a.** All wage labour in the business sector is productive. It serves as a comparison with conventional measures.
