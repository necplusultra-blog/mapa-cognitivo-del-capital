Cognitive Map of Contemporary Capitalism

The aim of this cognitive map of capitalism is to give an account of the fundamental tendencies of contemporary capitalism. It does so along two paths that converge and intersect: the study of a wide variety of empirical data —national statistics on employment, hours of labour, capital, trade and input–output tables, as well as official statistics on the same matters from international bodies such as the OECD, the UN and the ILO—, translated into the categories of the critique of political economy, and the confrontation of the main contemporary Marxist readings that investigate those same data.

The theoretical foundation of this project lies, consequently, in Marx's critique of political economy —unfolded by Marx in writings such as Capital and the Grundrisse, as well as in the various manuscripts linked to his project of an immanent critique of capitalist society— and in a critical appropriation of its various contemporary readings —such as, for example, those of Moishe Postone, Michael Heinrich, Robert Kurz and Michael Roberts, among others—.

This project is called Cognitive Map of Capital because it is a critical cartography of capitalist social relations on a global scale. What is at stake here is to record the empirical manifestations of the fundamental tendencies of the capitalist mode of production —its crises included—, with the aim of contributing a critical reading of contemporary capitalism and of bringing to light the possibilities of determinate negation that are developing within it today.

As Fredric Jameson (1988, p. 353) argued, the elaboration of a cognitive map of our historical moment, which implies a cognition and a critical representation of capitalist relations on a planetary scale, is an integral part of any political project oriented towards the emancipatory transformation of existing society. That is precisely the task to which this cognitive map is devoted.

This project records the rate and the mass of profit and of surplus value as a complex multiverse of specifications, linking the study of each statistical variable analysed to an explicit theoretical decision, and it is updated whenever the sources publish new data. It also records the state of the productive forces that capital develops: how much labour time is needed to reproduce those who produce, and what capital turns the time freed by that development into. Contained in that conversion, as an objective possibility, is disposable time.

With this cognitive map, as with my research, I seek to take up and continue the necessarily unfinished project of Marx's critique of political economy, not to do critical political economy. As Dr Alfonso García Vela pointed out to me, in a project of this kind it is important to avoid relapsing into a mere Marxist economics, into a critical political economy, since, although the quantitative question carries enormous weight in capitalist society, what is fundamental in it is the category of capital —the logic of its concept, which determines its real movement—. That is why, in this project, the immanent critique of capitalist society comes first: profit is set out as a form of appearance of surplus value, surplus value is the unpaid labour of the productively exploited fraction of the working class, and the figures record the empirical manifestation —on the surface of society— of those deeper conceptual determinations; they do not demonstrate them.

Just as Marx, in his lifetime, processed an enormous quantity of economic data available in the British Museum in order to elaborate the concept of capital, the cognitive map uses artificial intelligence to process economic data and official statistical sources that today would be impossible for a single person —or even for a group of people devoted to the task— to gather by hand. It would be absurd, and counterproductive, for an immanent critique of established society to refuse to use the most advanced technological tools of our epoch, even while holding a critical perspective on the determinations of those tools as capital —that is, as technologies that in no way exist neutrally, but are constituted by capital—. The theoretical decisions that guide the analytical processing done by the machine are, however, explicit and documented at each fork. The full methodology brings together sources, formulae, decisions, splices and limitations, so that any researcher can audit the results. The first reading of the results interprets them from the standpoint of the Marxian theory of the immanent limits of capital that is the object of my research.

This cognitive map of contemporary capitalism is, in this sense, a project carried out within the broader framework of my doctoral thesis project, Los límites inmanentes del capital. Crisis, contratendencias y la hipótesis del capitalismo limítrofe (Doctorate in Sociology, ICSyH 'Alfonso Vélez Pliego', BUAP), supervised by Dr Alfonso García Vela, which I am developing thanks to a scholarship granted by Mexico's Secretariat of Science, Humanities, Technology and Innovation (SECIHTI), number 4052073. I am grateful to the Mexican working class, whose labour makes this scholarship possible, and I hope to be putting it to good use by producing knowledge oriented towards the radical transformation of society. — Pablo Ignacio Jiménez Cea

There is no single way of measuring the rate of profit: it depends on what is counted as profit and what as capital, and each theoretical current decides differently. Instead of choosing one, the cognitive map calculates all the reasonable combinations and draws them together. The line is the typical value; the bands show how far the measurements diverge from one another. If the band is narrow, the theories agree; if it is wide, the result depends on the theory. How is this chart calculated?

Each specification is an annual rate r = numerator / denominator. The numerator changes according to the fork: net surplus, profit before or after tax, or broad surplus value by industry. The denominator is the BEA's year-end net stock of fixed assets (K), at current or historical cost, with or without intellectual property products and inventories, and either all capital or only that of the productive industries (in that case, the depreciation of the unproductive industries is not subtracted from the numerator). In the business sector there is also a fork with variable capital in the denominator: pv/(K + V). The full list is in the methodology, §5.

To compare specifications at different levels, each series is divided by its period average and multiplied by 100. Then, for each year, the 10th, 25th, 50th, 75th and 90th percentiles of all the series are calculated. The fan is not a confidence interval: it shows how much the level of the rate depends on theoretical decisions.

Six readings in the debate faced with the same data

The Marxist debate on the rate of profit is not only about figures: it is about what profit is, what capital is and what it means for the rate to fall or rise. Here six currents are set to read the same data for the United States and the world. Each line in the chart is the rate calculated in the manner of one of them; each card, below, summarises what that current looks at, what it sees in these data and what diagnosis it draws. The lines do not coincide in level, because they count different things, but they do coincide in much of their movement: all of them fall until the early 1980s and none, in the broad measures of surplus value, returns to its post-war level. What it looks at. Surplus value in the broad sense: all the new value of the productive industries minus the wages of their workers, over fixed capital. It distinguishes productive and unproductive labour (Shaikh and Tonak, 1994).

Diagnosis. A long-run falling tendency that operates through competition between capitals and manifests itself in long waves. The neoliberal phase was a recovery of the profit of enterprise sustained by wage restraint and the fall in the rate of interest, and the 2008 crisis marked its exhaustion (Shaikh, 2016).

Moseley: the surplus value absorbed by unproductive labour

What it looks at. The rate of surplus value of production and non-supervisory workers in the productive industries, and the part of surplus value spent on circulation and supervisory labour (Moseley, 1991).

What it sees here. The surplus value produced and the profit appropriated diverge. The within-industry rate goes from —% in 1982 to — and stands at —%, above its 1974–1979 levels, while the Duménil-Lévy-type corporate rate remains below that of the 1960s.

Kliman: a rate that never recovered

What it looks at. Corporate profit over capital valued at what it actually cost (historical cost), in the temporal single-system interpretation (TSSI).

What it sees here. The rate falls from — and, since the 1980s, has moved well below its 1950s level: —% in 2019. The jump to —% after 2021 coincides with inflation, which inflates current profits relative to a capital valued at past prices.

Roberts and Carchedi: the law as the ultimate cause of crises

What it looks at. Surplus value over fixed capital plus variable capital, pv/(c + v), and the world rate of profit.

What it sees here. The US rate falls from —% in 1948 to —. The net world rate (Penn World Table) goes from —% in 2007 to —. The K/V composition rises again after 2000 (— → —), and in the phase beginning in 2004 the rate falls in all the business-sector specifications.

Diagnosis. The rise in the organic composition pushes the rate down in the long run; the countertendencies only check it for a time. Crises break out when the mass of profit falls, and since 2008 the world economy has been living through a ‘long depression’ (Roberts, 2016; Carchedi and Roberts, 2022).

Heinrich: there is no law that the data could confirm

What it looks at. It does not propose a measure. It reads the entire spread of the multiverse and asks whether the fall can be deduced from the determinations of capital.

What it sees here. Only — of the — changes between decades hold across all specifications. The K/V composition rose until 1982 (—), fell until 2000 owing to the cheapening of capital, and rose again. The level and the direction depend on the theory adopted.

Diagnosis. The law cannot be demonstrated in general: the same increase in productive force that raises the technical composition cheapens constant capital and raises the rate of surplus value, and the net effect remains indeterminate. Crises must be explained by credit, money and overaccumulation, not by a tendency of the rate (Heinrich, 2013).

How is each series reconstructed?
  • Shaikh and Tonak (1994, tables 5.6 and 5.8). S* = net product of the business sector (excluding rental housing) minus V*; V* = compensation of production and non-supervisory workers in the productive industries (the cognitive map's within-industry correction, from 1972). What households pay to finance is not subtracted, because they do not subtract it. The Marxian rate is S*/K, with K the net stock at current cost; they use the gross stock, which is no longer published, so only the trend is comparable. From 1948 there is a version with V* equal to all the compensation in the productive industries, which is a lower bound of their rate of surplus value.
  • Moseley (1991, ch. 3; 1977 figures in Moseley, 1997). V = production and non-supervisory wages in the productive industries; Uf = the rest of the business sector's compensation (circulation and supervision); C = fixed capital of the productive industries plus inventories; Us = fixed capital of trade, finance and insurance, non-residential real estate, rental and leasing, and legal services. Conventional rate = (S − Uf)/(C + Us), with S net of the business sector's taxes on production net of subsidies (NIPA 1.13, lines 9 and 17; see above).
  • Kliman (2010; 2012, ch. 5). Corporations (NIPA 1.14). Property income = net value added − compensation; total profit = net operating surplus; before-tax profit = profits with inventory valuation adjustment. In all three, depreciation at current cost is replaced by depreciation at historical cost, and the denominator is the net stock at historical cost at the end of the previous year (the capital advanced).
  • Duménil and Lévy (2002, table 3). Broad rate r_b = (net value added of corporations − compensation) / net non-residential stock at current cost. The ratio between years is compared, because the article does not publish annual levels. There is a variant without intellectual property products (neither their investment in the numerator nor their stock in the denominator), closer to the accounts they used.
  • Roberts (2018; 2021). Surplus value = national income − compensation of employees; C = private non-residential fixed assets, at historical cost and at current cost; v = compensation; rate = s/(C + v).
  • Result. For levels, ‘reproduces’ if the difference is up to 10% of the published value and ‘approximates’ if it is up to 25%. For changes, ‘reproduces’ if the difference is up to 5 points and the direction coincides, and ‘approximates’ if it is up to 12 points. Beyond that, it is indicated whether at least the direction coincides. The level of Shaikh and Tonak's Marxian rate is marked as not comparable, because they use the gross stock and the net stock is used here; for that rate the comparison of changes is what counts.

The BEA accounts are revised every year, and comprehensively every five. That is why an author's figures from 1994 or 2002 cannot coincide exactly with today's, even if the definition is the same. Details of the definitions and sources in the methodology.

Endogenous breaks

How many specifications detect a break in trend in each year. No break point is fixed in advance.

A statistical procedure searches, in each of the measurements of the multiverse, for the years in which the rate changes course, without anyone telling it where to look. Each bar counts how many measurements show a change of course in that year. The tall bars are the tremors that almost all of them record. They serve to test the periodisations of theory against what the data mark out on their own.

How are the breaks calculated?

Piecewise linear trends are fitted to each specification, with up to 5 breaks and a minimum segment length of h years, by dynamic programming (as in Bai and Perron, 2003). The number of breaks m is chosen by minimising BIC = n·ln(RSS/n) + k·ln(n), with k = 3m + 2. The chart adds up, year by year, how many specifications place a break in that year (baseline configuration: h = 8, BIC). The table repeats the calculation with h = 6 and h = 10, and with the more demanding LWZ criterion.

Which conclusions withstand all the decisions

For each step from one decade to the next, the table counts in what percentage of the measurements the rate falls. If it falls in almost all of them (or rises in almost all of them), the movement does not depend on the theory chosen; if it falls in some and rises in others, the conclusion depends on how it is measured. This is the way to separate what the data show from what theory brings to them.

The two-sided law: mass and rate of surplus value in the United States

Mass of surplus value in purchasing power (billions of 2017 USD)

Above, how much surplus value is produced in total each year, expressed in what that money can buy at 2017 prices. It is not a measure of value, which is measured in labour time (see the section in hours), but of the mass of use values that surplus value makes it possible to appropriate. Below, how much surplus value capital obtains for each dollar of wages paid to productive workers: the degree of exploitation expressed in money. The mass grows almost without pause and has multiplied more than eightfold since 1948. The rate of surplus value falls until 1982, stays almost unchanged until 2000 and has risen since then. Together with the rate of profit of the multiverse, they show the two-sided law of accumulation: the same dynamic that enlarges the mass of surplus value presses the rate of profit downwards, and capital responds by increasing exploitation.

From money to labour time: the United States

The mass of surplus value is the product of the rate of surplus value (pv/v, the degree of exploitation) and the number of productively exploited workers. Measured in money, the rate of surplus value of productive workers expresses the proportion between surplus labour and necessary labour in their working day: of each week of labour, a fraction 1/(1+e) reproduces the value of their labour power and a fraction e/(1+e) is surplus labour.

United States: the productive worker's week, divided into necessary labour and surplus labour (hours)

A full-time 40-hour week of a productive worker in the United States, split in two according to each year's rate of surplus value. One line is the hours in which the worker produces the equivalent of their wage (necessary labour); the other, the hours in which they work for capital without receiving anything in return (surplus labour). Necessary labour falls from 13.8 to 9.7 hours since 1972, at an uneven pace: it is compressed sharply between 2000 and 2007 and almost stalls afterwards. That it cannot reach zero is a property of the form of the working day, not an explanation of that pause, which has historical causes. The third line is the average paid week of all production workers, full-time and part-time: it is not a working day, and it falls mainly because industries with short working hours, such as hospitality, are growing.

Each year, the 40-hour week is divided according to that same year's rate of surplus value: necessary hours = 40 / (1 + e); surplus labour = 40 × e / (1 + e). The average paid week comes from the BLS establishment survey (CES).

How are these data obtained?

  2. Hours (H). For each supersector, employment × average weekly hours paid (series 07; in manufacturing, AWHMAN) × 52.
  3. Rate of surplus value (e). S/V from the previous section, in its within-industry variant: S is the net broad surplus value of the business sector and V the compensation of those same workers (employment × hourly wage × hours × 52, scaled up to include benefits).

  6. Decomposition. Between the first and the last year, each factor contributes ln(factorend/factorstart) / ln(massend/massstart); the three contributions add up to 100%.

The field of countertendencies

Marx (2021, pp. 297–307) sets out six causes that counteract the tendential fall of the rate of profit: the raising of the degree of exploitation, the reduction of wages below their value, the cheapening of the elements of constant capital, relative surplus population, foreign trade and the increase in share capital. They are not external forces that correct the tendency from outside: they are produced by the same causes that produce the fall. This section brings together observable indicators for each of them, and begins with the central question: in each historical stage of developed capitalism, what sustained or sank the rate of profit?

How is the transfer of hours calculated?

For each country, h = hours worked in the year (persons employed × annual hours per person employed, Penn World Table 11.0) / GDP in current dollars (World Bank). The average hour content of one dollar of world exports is hX = Σ exports × h / Σ exports. A country's net transfer is imports × hX − exports × h. The countries with data on hours are used (some 120, from 2005); the income groups are those of the World Bank.

Assumptions: what a country exports embodies the average hours per dollar of its economy, and what it imports, the average content of world exports; intermediate inputs are not traced, which would require a world input–output matrix; all hours worked are counted, not only productive ones. The transfer does not create surplus value: it redistributes it among capitals and national formations.

What the hours do not see: labour intensity and platform work

The whole cognitive map counts labour in hours: an hour is an hour. There are two movements of contemporary capital that this measure does not register well, and both point in the same direction: they make the measurement in hours underestimate surplus labour. This section does not correct the figures; it reports that bias, its direction and what is known about its size.

The intensity of labour

Alongside the prolongation of the working day and the increase in productive force, Marx distinguishes a third path to surplus value: intensification, that is, more labour compressed into the same time, with fewer pores in the working day (Marx, 2018, ch. XIII). A more intensive hour creates more value than an hour of normal intensity, but in the accounts it appears the same. If intensity rises, the rate of surplus value in hours falls short of the effective one. No statistic measures intensity directly; since 1991 the European working conditions surveys have asked whether people work at high speed or to tight deadlines. What they show is a strong intensification between 1991 and 2005 and a high plateau since then: a third of European workers work at high speed three-quarters of the time or more.

Platform work

Uber drivers, delivery riders and those who perform online tasks appear in the statistics as own-account (self-employed) workers. Under the cognitive map's general criterion, the income of own-account workers is not surplus value and they do not count as productive workers. But under the criterion of this research, platform work such as Uber's is productive labour in Marx's sense: the platform operates as a capitalist that subsumes the labour of the drivers. From the fare paid by the customer, it retains a commission—often between 20% and 40%—and hands the rest to the driver. That commission, after deducting the costs of servers, offices and software, is the form in which the surplus value extracted from living labour appears. The driver works one part of the working day to cover subsistence, petrol and the wear and tear of the car (necessary labour), and the rest for the platform (surplus labour). It is a form of piece wages (Marx, 2018, ch. XIX): it gives the appearance of greater freedom than a fixed wage and pushes workers themselves to lengthen and intensify their working day. The platform's capital—software, algorithms, brand—is valorised only when the driver logs on and makes the trip.

Because the statistics record them as own-account workers, the cognitive map's measurement leaves these workers out: here too the bias is downwards. Their weight is still small in the United States, but it is growing worldwide.

How far do prices depart from values?

For each industry we calculate how many hours of labour, direct and indirect, one dollar of its product contains: the hours worked in the industry itself plus those contained in the inputs it buys from the capitalist part of other productive industries, domestic or imported. The transformation according to Moseley, with the temporal variant (TSSI) as a contrast

The previous measurement calculates values in hours. This one follows the interpretation of Moseley (2016), which the cognitive map adopts as central: the money costs—inputs and wages—are the same for values and for prices of production, and what changes is only how surplus value is distributed. In values, each industry would have the surplus value produced by its own workers; in prices of production, each industry receives a profit proportional to the capital it advances. The difference is the surplus value that competition shifts from some industries to others. The temporal variant (TSSI; Kliman, 2007) values inputs and capital at the prices at which they entered production: in years of inflation it gives higher rates, and in the 2009 crisis it converges with the central one.

  2. Industries that do not produce value. φ = 1 in the productive industries and 0 in trade, finance, legal and accounting services, advertising, real estate, public administration and households: their inputs do not transfer value; they are expenses paid out of surplus value (the treatment of Shaikh and Tonak, 1994). Secondary exploitation: what households pay to finance

The value added of banks and insurers enters the accounts as if it were all produced profit. But a growing part of it is paid by households—implicit interest on loans and deposits, fees—out of wages that have already been counted as variable capital. That is not new surplus value, but surplus value displaced from the wage to financial capital: what Marx called secondary exploitation (Capital, Volume III, ch. XXXVI). The cognitive map subtracts it from surplus value in the part that corresponds to wages and records it separately, as an indicator of the transfer and anticipation of surplus value. In the United States, this displacement went from a minimal fraction of surplus value in the post-war period to a considerable part. If it were left in, as in the accounts, the rate of profit would be higher and its recovery since 1982 would appear larger.

Supervision and management

Marx distinguishes (Capital, Volume III, ch. XXIII) the work of direction, necessary in all cooperation, from the work of superintendence, which arises from the antagonism between capital and labour. The statistics do not separate them. The site uses two bounds: the by-industry criterion counts all the wage earners of the productive industries, managers included, as variable capital; the within-industry criterion counts only production and non-supervisory workers, and leaves supervisory salaries within surplus value. The second line of the previous chart shows that the distance between the two bounds is growing: the share of compensation that goes to supervisors, managers and non-productive staff within the productive industries has been rising since the 1970s. It is the phenomenon that Duménil and Lévy (2011) read as the rise of the managerial cadres, and Moseley (1991) as the growth of unproductive labour.

Net social wage: what the state gives back and what it takes

The state levies taxes and contributions on workers and gives them back benefits: pensions, unemployment insurance, public health, education. If it gave back more than it takes, it would be shifting to the wage part of the surplus value it collects from capitals; if it gives back less, it is the workers themselves who finance their social security. Shaikh and Tonak (1994) call that difference the net social wage. In the United States it is almost always negative: workers pay, as a whole, more than they receive (on average, 4% of their compensation; 9% in the year 2000). Only after the 2008 crisis and during the pandemic was it positive. That is why it does not change the conclusions, and the site shows it as a sensitivity analysis.

World: commercial rent and household financial services (billion hours of surplus labour)

At the world scale, the central measurement adds the rent that firms pay for their premises (surplus value ceded to landed property) and subtracts the financial services that households pay for out of their wages. The two adjustments go in opposite directions and almost cancel each other out: the world mass of surplus labour changes very little relative to the national-accounts convention.

How are these adjustments calculated?
  • B4, secondary exploitation (in the central measurement). United States: from broad surplus value is subtracted personal consumption expenditure on financial services (NIPA, table 2.4.5, series DFNLRC: implicit interest—FISIM—plus fees) multiplied by the share of employee compensation in personal income (A033RC / A065RC), so as not to subtract what households pay out of property income. World: household final consumption of finance and insurance products (K64–K66, domestic and imported; WIOD input–output tables) is subtracted, as a proportion of the value added of the market industries, multiplied by the labour share of income (Penn World Table, labsh). The corporate measures ‘in the manner of’ Duménil-Lévy and Kliman do not change.
  • B3, commercial rent (in the central measurement, world only). The intermediate consumption of real estate products (L68) by the market industries is added, as a proportion of their value added (WIOD). In the United States this is not needed: business-sector surplus value already includes commercial real estate and excludes only housing.
  • Application to the world. For each country, e' = e + (B3 − B4) × (market value added / v); surplus labour = hours × e' / (1 + e'). After 2014 each country's 2014 proportions are kept.
  • B2, supervision. 1 − within-industry v / by-industry v: the share of the compensation of the productive industries that does not correspond to production and non-supervisory workers. It includes supervisors and managers, but also differences in coverage between the establishment survey (BLS) and the industry accounts (BEA): it is an approximation.
  • B5, net social wage (sensitivity, from 1959). Benefits received by wage-earning households = (government social benefits, A063RC, + current public expenditure on education, NIPA 3.16) × compensation / personal income. Taxes they pay = personal taxes (W055RC) × compensation / personal income + contributions for social insurance (A061RC; employer contributions are already part of compensation). Net social wage = benefits − taxes; the share of productive workers is proportional to their compensation. Variant: e = (S − SSN) / (V + SSN). It is an approximation: it allocates benefits and taxes using the same wage share of personal income.

Scope and sources
