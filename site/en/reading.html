A first reading of the results

What follows is a first critical interpretation of the data produced by the cognitive map when they are read from the standpoint of the Marxian theory of the immanent limits of capital. The aim is to give an account of the fundamental tendencies of contemporary capitalist production: what form the rate and the mass of surplus value, exploitation, the composition of capital and the field of its countertendencies —or counteracting causes of the tendential fall of the rate of profit— take today; how the main Marxist currents read them; and what objective possibilities are contained in the development of the productive forces driven by capital under the blind compulsion to the valorisation of value. It is not a summary of figures but a critical reading of them: the figures correspond to the November 2026 measurement and may change when the sources used publish new data.

An interpretation developed within the framework of Pablo Ignacio Jiménez Cea's doctoral research, Los límites inmanentes del capital (SECIHTI scholarship number 4052073), and of his article 'The immanent dialectic of capital' (Capital & Class, 2026). Preliminary version.

Critique of political economy, not critical political economy

This work seeks to take up and continue the necessarily unfinished project of Marx's critique of political economy. Unfinished, because Marx left Volumes II and III of Capital as incomplete manuscripts, along with manuscripts and drafts of the project of a critique of political economy, written before and after his magnum opus, that run to thousands of pages. It is a necessarily unfinished project, moreover, because its object is not a fixed structure but a historically specific movement of social production that continually transforms the world while reconstituting the relations of production immanent to it: the immanent critique of capitalist society must therefore continue that project by studying the fundamental tendencies of contemporary capitalism.

The qualitative comes first

There is a difference of principle between the critique of political economy and a critical political economy. The latter takes the categories of economics —profit, wage, capital— as given transhistorical forms, while it discusses their measurement or their quantitative distribution. The former, by contrast, asks why the product of labour takes the form of the commodity, of value and of capital. For it, these categories are forms of a historically specific social relation: socially valid, and therefore objective, forms of thought, which impose themselves on individuals as if they were natural properties of things (Marx, 2018, pp. 91–93). Value, surplus value and capital are not, in this sense, quantities to be found immediately in official economic statistics. They are categories of a determinate mode of social relations, of social praxis, which correspond to the fundamental relations of production of capitalist society. They are consequently categories of the critique of political economy because, as the immanent critique of capitalist society, it gives an account of those fundamental relations and reveals the categories of political economy to be necessary forms of appearance of those social relations and practices on the surface of society.

That reality has, as Dr Alfonso García Vela has pointed out to me, a quantitative dimension of enormous force. Value exists only as a category of the totality and, as abstract wealth grounded in labour time, as a determinate quantitative magnitude: as socially necessary labour time, as money, as the rate of profit, and so on. Yet if measurement were to become independent of the critical category, the critique would relapse into mere Marxist economics: the rate of profit becomes a thermometer whose trajectory would by itself decide the fate of capitalism. This is the question shared, though they give opposite answers to it, by Robert Kurz's breakdown reading and by Michael Heinrich's reading of the indeterminacy of the tendential fall of the rate of profit. The cognitive map seeks to avoid that relapse through four rules:

  1. The categories of the critique of political economy decide what is counted. Which labour is productive, what enters capital, whether households' payments to finance are surplus value: none of these questions is settled statistically. They are theoretical and categorial decisions, and the site makes them explicit at each fork.
  2. Statistical series show; they do not demonstrate. They record the phenomenal manifestation of fundamental determinations of capital set out by Marxian theory; they neither prove those determinations nor measure the distance to any limit.
  3. Measuring in time is part of the critique. The cognitive map expresses wealth in hours of labour not because time is a natural measure of wealth, but because it is the measure that capital imposes on it. What is measured is the object of the critique, not a neutral yardstick.
  4. What is decisive are the transformations and tendencies of contemporary capitalism. The transformations that matter —secondary exploitation through finance, artificial intelligence as a general condition of production, the disposable time that capital turns into surplus labour— are changes in the qualitative articulation of the field of countertendencies. The figures serve to recognise the empirical way in which the fundamental tendencies of capitalist production manifest themselves, not as a substitute for their immanent critique.

Why use artificial intelligence

Marx did not elaborate the concept of capital through the abstractions of his thought alone. The concept of capital in Marx is the result of a long theoretical practice, full of discoveries, dead ends, abandoned drafts and the continual concretisation of the categories. To that end he processed an enormous quantity of empirical material: the reports of the factory inspectors and the blue books of the British Parliament on which the chapter on the working day in Capital rests (Marx, 2018), the notebooks in which he recorded the world crisis of 1857 day by day (Marx, 2017) and, in the 1870s, extensive studies of the agrarian statistics of Russia and the United States, of banking and of crises (Heinrich, 2013). That empirical work did not illustrate a critical category elaborated in advance: it was part of his elaboration and continual concretisation of the concept.

Today the official sources —national accounts, input–output tables, employment and hours surveys from dozens of countries over more than seventy years— exceed by several orders of magnitude what a researcher could process by hand. Artificial intelligence makes it possible to gather, harmonise and recompute them, and to leave every step in view so that anyone can audit it. With it, the continuation of Marx's theoretical project can be combined with the study of how the fundamental tendencies of capital are expressed empirically in the material development of capitalist society, drawing on the largest possible quantity of data and official sources.

The tool, however, decides nothing. The labour of the concept and metacognition remain human. The categories, the criteria and the theoretical interpretation of the data are mine, and the code and the decisions are public so that anyone can test the evidence, enrich it or point out possible errors in my analysis. There is, moreover, a reflexivity that should not be lost from view: artificial intelligence is one of the productive forces whose capitalist form this very cognitive map analyses. Using it for the critique means employing a power of the social intellect against the form in which it is presented to society: as capital.

What can be asked of a measurement

National accounts do not record surplus value, variable capital or productive labour. They record prices, wages, jobs and stocks valued in money. Translating them into categories of the critique of political economy requires theoretical decisions, and that is why the cognitive map does not offer a single measurement of the rate of profit, but a multiverse of specifications in which each fork corresponds to an explicit theoretical decision. What withstands all the different theoretical decisions can be read as a manifestation of the dynamics of capital; what changes with them shows where theory decides the result and the way in which that tendency is read.

To clarify, and to be more explicit about the project's methodology, this means that there is no single correct way of measuring the rate of profit, so the project does not give a single measurement parameter. Indeed, any attempt to measure the rate of profit requires several theoretical decisions before calculating. For example:

  • which workers count as productive;
  • whether capital is valued at what it cost or at what it would cost today;
  • whether depreciation is deducted;
  • whether, for example, software, research and intellectual property count as capital.

Each possible answer to each question leads down a different path, and that is a 'fork'. So, given the enormous quantity of data at my disposal, instead of choosing one path and hiding the others, I arranged for the project to compute the rate along all the paths at once, using artificial intelligence.

'Specification', for its part, is the technical term for a complete and precise way of calculating something. A specification of the rate of profit is defined once everything has been fixed:

  • what goes into the numerator and the denominator;
  • with which data it is calculated;
  • which kinds of labour count as producing surplus value;
  • how capital is valued.

If a single one of those decisions changes, the result is another specification. Combining these decisions yields 288 different versions of the calculation of the rate of profit, and that is the 'multiverse' of the rate of profit. Each version states which decisions it made, so that no one auditing this project has to guess where each number comes from.

The idea of a 'multiverse' comes from social-science methodology. Steegen, Tuerlinckx, Gelman and Vanpaemel proposed it in 2016 as 'multiverse analysis'. The core of their argument is that when a study presents a single result, it hides all the other reasonable decisions it could have made, and with them all the possible results. Each combination of theoretical decisions in a study would produce a possible 'universe' of results, so showing them all together at the same time, as a multiverse, lets the rest of the research community see clearly which conclusions hold in every universe and which depend on a particular choice.

In this project of cognitive mapping of contemporary capitalism and its fundamental tendencies, then, 'multiverse of specifications' means the set of 288 ways of calculating, in the first place, the US rate of profit that result from combining the theoretical decisions disputed among the Marxist currents. None is presented as the true one. What matters is what they all show together: for example, the post-war fall to the mid-1970s, which appears in almost all of them. I chose that country because, although I would have liked to complement it with China, I unfortunately did not have as much data on China as on the United States, since much of it is not published by the country.

Finally, the caution that led me to choose this method has a deeper ground than statistical prudence. If the immanent limits of capital were some kind of reachable ceiling, a well-made measurement could say how close the system is to reaching it. But the limit that the capital relation cannot cross —the reduction to zero of the labour necessary for the reproduction of the working class— has the form of an asymptote: capital approaches it without being able to reach it, because reaching it would abolish the relation itself (Marx, 2018, p. 741; 2016a, pp. 282–284). No statistical series, then, measures a 'distance' to the limit. What can be observed is the form in which capital processes its limits today: with what rates of exploitation, what masses of labour, what composition of capital and what countertendencies. The series show the phenomenal manifestation of those determinations; they do not demonstrate them by themselves.

A two-sided law: the rate of profit does not collapse and the mass of surplus value grows

The first result is negative and, for that very reason, theoretically decisive. In the United States, the multiverse median goes from 121 in 1948 to 90 in 1982 and returns to 120 in 2025 (100 = average for the period). The robustness table shows that only two changes between decades withstand all the specifications: the fall from the 1940s to the 1950s and the rise of the 2020–2025 stretch relative to the previous decade. All other movements depend on the theory adopted. No specification describes a linear and irreversible fall of the system.

Meanwhile, the mass of surplus value of the business sector, measured in purchasing power, increases more than eightfold: from 972 billion 2017 dollars in 1948 to 8,277 billion in 2025. This is the empirical form of what Marx called a two-sided law (Marx, 2021, pp. 277–280; Jiménez Cea, 2026): the same laws of accumulation produce a growing absolute mass of surplus value and a tendentially falling rate of profit. A collapsist reading, which would deduce an exhaustion of the mass of surplus value from the relative expulsion of living labour, finds no support in this empirical measurement. Nor does the opposite image of a valorisation without limits, as will be seen further on when observing what sustains that mass.

The movement of the rate can be read through the Marxian decomposition r = e · v/K. With the by-industry criterion, the composition K/V rises from 2.96 in 1948 to 4.00 in 1982, while the rate of surplus value falls from 1.27 to 1.03: in 1982 the highest composition up to then coincides with the lowest exploitation of the period, and the rate reaches its minimum. Since then the recovery has rested on the two levers that Marx identifies as countertendencies: the composition falls to 3.32 by 2000 —cheapening of the elements of constant capital— and the rate of surplus value rises to 1.23 in 2025; with the within-industry correction, from 2.16 in 1982 to 3.14. The composition rises again after 2000 (3.92 in 2025), so that the rate is sustained only because exploitation keeps increasing.

The fork in the denominator adds a nuance. With broad surplus value by industry divided by fixed capital alone, the 2025 rate is 27% below that of 1948; divided by total capital advanced, pv/(c + v), it is 22% below. The multiverse median recovers its post-war level because it includes specifications of appropriated profit (corporate surplus, after-tax profit) that recovered more than the broad measures of surplus value. The difference between the two families is a datum in itself: the recovery since 1982 is more intense in the profit that is appropriated than in the surplus value that is produced.

Six readings of the same data

The currents in the Marxist debate on the rate of profit are not distinguished only by their definitions. Each isolates one moment of the movement that the series record and turns it into the centre of its diagnosis. Having them read the same data —the chart and cards on the main page— makes it possible to see what each one captures and what it leaves out. They are grouped here by the moment they privilege.

The tendency: Kliman, Roberts and Carchedi

Kliman (2012), Roberts (2016) and Carchedi and Roberts (2018; 2022) read the series as confirmation of a long-term fall. The data bear them out on one decisive point. In the broad measures of surplus value, the US rate in 2025 is still below that of 1948: by 27% with fixed capital, by 22% with total capital advanced. The net world rate from the Penn World Table falls from 8.5% in 2007 to 5.2% in 2023. And between 2004 and 2016 the rate falls in all the business specifications of the multiverse. Recomputed with each author’s definition and current data (the recomputed series), the changes Roberts (2018) published are reproduced almost exactly: −20% between 1965 and 1982 and +8% between 1982 and 1997, at historical cost. And measured by property income or by total profit over capital at historical cost, as Kliman measures it, the corporate rate does not recover between 1982 and 1997 (−13% and −15%).

But the same data contradict the form these readings tend to give the tendency. Between 1982 and 2000 the composition of capital falls and the rate rises in the current-cost measures (not in Kliman’s, at historical cost, which falls). The real mass of US surplus value increases more than eightfold since 1948, and the world mass of surplus labour grows by 37% between 2000 and 2025. The historical-cost rate that Kliman privileges goes from 16.8% in 2019 to 23.4% in 2025, largely because of inflation, which inflates current profit relative to a capital valued at past prices. The no-recovery thesis, moreover, depends on the measure: over the same period, pre-tax profit over capital at historical cost rises by 8%. And a good part of the fall in the world rate occurs in China, where accumulation is greatest. A ‘long depression’ in which the mass of exploited surplus labour keeps growing is a characterisation that is hard to sustain. The fall in the rate is not the halting of accumulation but the form accumulation takes when it accelerates.

The countertendency: Duménil and Lévy, Heinrich

Duménil and Lévy (2004; 2011) and Heinrich (2013; 2014) place the emphasis on the recovery and on the indeterminacy of the movement. These readings, too, capture something the series show unambiguously. The rate recovers after 1982 in the measures of surplus value and of business profit (in those of the Duménil–Lévy and Kliman type, only after 2001), and that recovery coincides with a redistribution against workers: the within-industry rate of surplus value rises from 2.16 in 1982 to 3.14 in 2025. Heinrich is right that the composition does not always rise: the cheapening of the elements of constant capital made it fall for two decades. And the spread of the multiverse shows how much of the level and direction of the rate depends on the theory adopted.

The replication, however, introduces a correction to this reading. With today’s data, the recovery of the broad corporate rate between 1982 and 2000, which Duménil and Lévy (2002) measured at 45%, is 13% (17% without intellectual property). The neoliberal restoration of profitability was, up to 2000, weaker than the accounts of the time showed; the corporate rate rises mainly afterwards.

But if the movement were indeterminate, or purely political, nothing should withstand all the specifications. What does withstand them is significant. When the rate of surplus value stopped rising, between 2004 and 2016, the rate of profit fell in all of them, under both criteria of productive labour. The neoliberal recovery was not a self-sustaining order but a countertendency that runs up against the form of the working day: the rate of surplus value can keep rising but, in that form, each increase compresses necessary labour less (a determination of the form, not a tendency the series show decade by decade).

Distribution: Shaikh and Moseley

The readings of Shaikh and Tonak (1994), Shaikh (2016) and Moseley (1991) make visible the difference between surplus value produced and profit appropriated, and the data confirm it. Since the 1982 trough, non-financial corporate profit recovers more (+47%) than broad surplus value by industry (+22%). The within-industry rate of profit exceeds its 1974–1979 levels (not those of 1972–1973), and the within-industry rate of surplus value exceeds those of the whole decade, while the corporate rate does not return to its 1960s levels. That difference is the space occupied by unproductive labour, interest and rent. The replication confirms it: Shaikh and Tonak’s rate of surplus value, recomputed with the wages of production workers, reproduces the one they published for 1975–1982 and, without deducting what households pay to finance, keeps rising to 3.28 in 2025. The ratio of unproductive to productive labour that Moseley (1997) gives for 1977 (0.94) is the same with today’s data, and rises to 1.54 in 2025. The scope of these readings has a limit: distribution does not create surplus value. The relative slowdown of unproductive expenditure and the fall in interest enlarge the appropriated share of a mass whose growth depends on something else.

A critical reading: two sides of one and the same movement

The six readings isolate moments of what, in Marx’s exposition, is a single contradictory movement. The law of the tendential fall of the rate of profit is two-sided (Marx, 2021, pp. 277–280; Jiménez Cea, 2026): the same laws of accumulation produce a rate subject to downward pressure and a growing absolute mass of surplus value. The countertendencies are not external factors that correct the tendency from outside, but the form in which the tendency is realised. Read in this way, the series cease to be divided between a ‘tendency’ confirmed by some and a ‘recovery’ confirmed by others. What they show is the way in which capital processes its immanent limits.

Neither collapse nor unlimited valorisation. The mass of surplus value grows in the United States and in the world, and the rate does not recover its post-war level in the broad measures. The readings of the tendency see only the first half of this sentence; those of the countertendency, only the second.

Exploitation grows asymptotically. At the centre, the decisive countertendency is the raising of the rate of surplus value. In a 40-hour week, the necessary labour of the US productive worker goes from 13.8 hours in 1972 to 9.7 in 2025. In the form of the working day, each hour subtracted from necessary labour increases surplus labour in a smaller proportion than the previous one; the series do not show diminishing returns decade by decade (the compression of 2000–2007 was faster than that of 1982–2000). Heinrich is right that the rate of surplus value has no finite maximum. What does have a limit is the surplus labour that fits into a working day, because necessary labour cannot reach zero without abolishing the capital relation. That the fall in the rate of profit cannot be deduced in general does not make indeterminate the form in which capital strains against that limit: the 2004–2016 phase shows what happens when that countertendency comes to a halt.

The mass is sustained by simultaneous working days. 94% of the growth in the US mass of surplus labour since 1972 is explained by the increase in the number of productive workers. On the world scale, the rate of surplus value in hours does not rise, and the mass grows through proletarianisation, above all in China and India. This is the other limit of the mass of surplus value: the number of working days that capital manages to set to work at the same time. Reabsorbing population requires ever more capital per worker, and the world mass net of depreciation has stagnated since 2018.

Distribution transfers and anticipates surplus value. The difference between surplus value produced and profit appropriated that Shaikh, Moseley and Duménil and Lévy see is the terrain of a third mode of the countertendencies. Foreign trade transfers hours of labour towards high-income countries. Households pay to finance, out of their wages, a growing portion of their income: surplus value displaced from the wage, not produced, which the cognitive map no longer counts as surplus value. A handful of artificial intelligence corporations intercept almost a fifth of US corporate profits. And the equities of non-financial corporations go from 35% to 216% of GDP: claims on surplus labour that has not yet been produced. None of these movements by itself increases the global mass of surplus value.

What these four observations allow us to determine is not how close capital is to a ceiling. That question is shared —even if they answer it in opposite ways— by the readings of exhaustion and those of indeterminacy. What can be determined is how the entire field of countertendencies is articulated today: with an exploitation that strains against a working day whose form limits what each compression yields, a mass that depends on reabsorbing population with more capital per worker, and an appropriation that grows faster than production. The hypothesis of borderline capitalism is a hypothesis about that articulation. This site does not demonstrate it: it shows its phenomenal manifestation and submits it to testing.

Necessary labour: a barrier that can only be strained

Critical analysis in labour time makes visible what the rate in money conceals. The rate of surplus value of production and non-supervisory workers employed by productive capital in the United States tells us in what proportion their working day is divided between necessary labour and surplus labour. For a full-time working day in a 40-hour week:

The last row is not a working day. It is the average of paid hours of all production workers, full-time and part-time. It falls mainly because the composition of employment changes: the week is 41 hours in manufacturing and 40 in construction, but 24 in leisure and hospitality, which weighs ever more heavily. Moreover, these are hours paid, not worked: they include paid holidays and exclude unpaid overtime.

In a 40-hour working day, the rate of surplus value rises by 66% and surplus labour grows by 4.2 hours in a little over half a century, while necessary labour is compressed from 13.8 to 9.7 hours. Surplus labour can grow only at the expense of necessary labour, and the latter can never reach zero. The multiplier of the productive force does not multiply surplus value: it divides necessary labour, and each division yields fewer hours than the previous one (Marx, 2016a, pp. 278–284). If the productivity of the labour that produces the means of subsistence were to double again, weekly surplus labour would grow by 4.8 hours; in 1972, the same doubling would have added 6.9. This is a property of the form of the working day, not a regularity that the series show decade by decade: the compression of necessary labour was 0.12 hours per year between 1972 and 1982, 0.09 between 1982 and 2000, 0.14 between 2000 and 2007, and only 0.02 between 2007 and 2025.

This does not indicate how long it will be before capitalism collapses, because that is a measurement made impossible by the very concept of capital. It indicates, rather, the asymptotic form in which capital strains against its immanent barriers. The rate of surplus value can keep rising without a finite maximum, precisely because its denominator approaches zero without reaching it, while the surplus labour that fits into a bounded working day grows less and less. Today surplus labour occupies 76% of the productive worker’s working day; in 1972 it occupied 65%.

The other path of the exploitation of surplus labour, the absolute extension of the working day, does not appear in these series: paid hours per worker fall, partly because of composition. But the paid hours of the establishment survey do not record the intensification of labour, platform work or the dissolution of the boundary between working time and life time that accompanies teleworking and algorithmic management. That dimension of absolute surplus value, which the Capital & Class article places at the centre of the first countertendency, requires indicators that this measurement does not yet have.

Productive forces and disposable time: the possibility that capital creates and converts into surplus labour

The cognitive map has a use that measurements of the rate of profit usually leave out. It records the state of the productive forces that capital develops and, with it, the objective possibilities contained in the materiality of capitalist society. Capitalist production is, at the same time, a process of valorisation and a material process of reproduction of social life. In order to produce relative surplus value, capital develops the productive force of social labour and reduces the time necessary to reproduce those who produce. In the Grundrisse, Marx condenses the twofold character of this movement: the tendency of capital is ‘on the one side to create disposable time, on the other side to convert it into surplus labour’ (Marx, 2016b, p. 232).

What the data show

In the United States, with 2025 productivity, the real wage that a productive worker earned in 1972 would be reproduced in 6.8 hours per week. Their actual necessary labour today is 9.7 hours. The difference, 2.9 hours, is what half a century of development of the productive force has returned to them as greater consumption. In a 40-hour working day, the time freed relative to 1972 is 7.1 hours per week: 59% was converted into surplus labour and 41% into consumption. None of it was converted into a shorter working day, because the normal 40-hour working day is the same one that has been in force since 1940. Capital does not shorten the working day: it reduces the part of it that belongs to the worker.

On the world scale, the same relation takes another form. In the 42 countries measured industry by industry, which together account for 60% of the world’s population, all production of surplus value rests on some 74 minutes a day of productive labour per inhabitant. Of these, 24 minutes reproduce the productive workers themselves and 50 are surplus labour (with the gross rate of surplus value; with the rate net of depreciation, 31 and 43). This time does not diminish: it grows from 65 minutes in 2000, because productive wage earners go from 19% to 22% of the population. At the centre, the time that the productive force frees is converted into surplus labour of the same workers. On the world scale, it is converted, in addition, into the incorporation of more population into labour for capital.

What these figures are not

They are not a calculation of how much each person would work in another society. A society of associated producers would also need labour to replace and expand the means of production, for care, health, education and common administration, and none of that enters into the productive labour for capital measured here. In such a society, moreover, necessary labour would have another content: it would no longer be determined by the wage of a class but, as Marx writes, ‘will find its measure in the needs of the social individual’ (Marx, 2016b, p. 232).

What the figures do show is the determinate form of a contradiction. By reducing necessary labour, capital creates time that could exist as disposable time for each individual and for society. But it cannot free it as such, because its wealth is the appropriation of others’ surplus labour time: it must maintain that time as surplus labour, and the necessary labour it ceaselessly reduces is, at the same time, the condition of its valorisation. It is the same barrier that the previous section showed in the productive worker’s working day: capital strains against it, but cannot cross it without abolishing itself. Marx formulates it in Book I: only ‘the abolition of the capitalist form of production allows the working day to be restricted to necessary labour’ (Marx, 2018, p. 642).

The productive forces are not capital in themselves

Machinery, science applied to production and, today, artificial intelligence and robotisation are the material support of that possibility. Under the capital relation they appear as powers of capital confronting the producers, and the section on artificial intelligence shows the extent to which they function as means of appropriation. But they are, above all, results of the combined social praxis of humanity, which exist as capital only within historically specific relations of production. When this development is measured in labour time, what measurement in money conceals comes into view: a society capable of reproducing its material wealth with a diminishing fraction of the time of those who produce, and organised so that this time does not belong to them.

The measurement does not indicate that this possibility will be realised, nor when. No statistical series measures the distance to the negation of capital. What it shows is that the possibility exists, that it grows with the development of the productive force and that the capital relation blocks it at every step. That disposable time should cease to exist in antithesis to surplus labour is not something capital can bring about: only the praxis of those who today reproduce that relation can do so.

What sustains the mass: simultaneous working days

If surplus labour per worker hardly grows, where does the multiplication of the mass come from? The decomposition of the annual US mass of surplus labour between 1972 and 2025 is conclusive: it is multiplied by 2.21, and 94% of that growth (in logarithms) is explained by the increase in the number of productive workers, which goes from 33.5 to 70.5 million. The share of surplus labour in the working day contributes 19%, and the reduction in average paid hours subtracts 12%.

The mass of surplus value is the product of two factors, and both are limited (Marx, 2018, pp. 368–373). The first, the rate of exploitation, moves within the asymptotic form just seen. The second is the number of simultaneous working days that capital manages to set to work, and it is limited by the exploitable working population. The measurement shows that, over the last half-century, the US mass of surplus value has been sustained above all by this second factor: by the incorporation of new fractions of the population into productive labour, not by an increase in the surplus labour each worker yields.

The world scale confirms the pattern and radicalises it. In the 42 countries measured industry by industry, productive wage earners go from 767 million in 2000 to 1,077 million in 2025, and the gross annual mass of surplus labour from 1,077 to 1,476 billion hours (+37%). The world rate of surplus value in hours, by contrast, barely moves: 2.05 in 2000, 2.10 in 2014, 2.07 in 2025. The world mass grows through proletarianisation, not through an increase in the average degree of exploitation. China and India today account for two thirds of that mass (33% and 32%): the expanded reproduction of world capital rests on the absorption of a relatively surplus population, which the same processes of accumulation had released from earlier forms of labour, in industries and regions of lower composition.

The version net of depreciation adds the other side. If the consumption of fixed capital is subtracted from surplus value, the world rate of surplus value falls from 1.67 in 2000 to 1.37 in 2025. Each hour of productive labour consumes a growing mass of fixed capital, and that portion of the new value is not available as surplus value. This is the form in which the tendency for ever more capital to be needed to set the same mass of living labour in motion manifests itself, on the world scale and in labour time. The fall is greater since 2008 and is concentrated in China and India, where depreciation comes from the Penn World Table and not from a measurement by industry; it is the most uncertain part of this reading.

Two movements are left out of this account, and both bias it downwards. The first is intensification: more labour compressed into the same hour (Marx, 2018, ch. XIII), which hours do not record and which European surveys show rising until 2005 and on a high plateau thereafter (Eurofound, 2007; 2025). The second is platform work. Statistics record drivers and couriers as own-account workers, but the platform subsumes them as a capitalist does: it retains part of each fare and appropriates the surplus labour of the person driving, in the form of piece wages (Marx, 2018, ch. XIX), which presents as freedom what is in fact a working day that the worker lengthens and intensifies themselves. It is another way of absorbing surplus population without the legal form of the wage. Its weight is still small in the United States (around 1% of employment in 2017; BLS, 2018), but the World Bank estimates between 154 and 435 million online platform workers worldwide (World Bank, 2023). The site records both biases without correcting the figures (What the hours do not see).

The world rate: the fall is concentrated where accumulation is greatest

With the Penn World Table, the net world rate of profit (total economy, capital-weighted, panel of 79 countries since 1960) fluctuates between 7.6% and 9.1% from 1960 to 2007 and falls to 5.2% in 2023. Without China, the fall is smaller: to 6.0%. Over the same period, China goes from holding 2% of world capital to holding 27%, and the United States from 39% to 13%. The world mass of gross profit, meanwhile, is multiplied almost twelvefold between 1960 and 2023.

The reading is consistent with that of the two previous sections. The most intense phase of the fall coincides with the most rapid accumulation in the history of capital, concentrated in the region that incorporated the most labour power into capitalist production. The fall in the rate does not occur where capital stagnates, but where it expands most: not because labour becomes less productive, but because it becomes more productive (Marx, 1968, p. 439). That the mass keeps growing at the same time shows that the movement is not one of exhaustion, but of reconstitution of the capital relation on a broader material basis and with a higher composition.

Values, prices and the world market

The formation of prices neither creates nor destroys surplus value: it redistributes it. The input–output tables of 43 countries make it possible to see this industry by industry between 2000 and 2014. Market prices deviate from values by 28% on average, and the direction of the deviation is the one Marx anticipated (Marx, 2021, Part Two): in almost all countries, industries with more capital per unit of labour sell above their value and those with more living labour, below it. Agriculture, fishing, textiles and hotels and restaurants cede value; petroleum refining, mining, energy, metals and chemicals appropriate it. The equalisation of profit rates is a mechanism of transfer from where more living labour is exploited to where more capital is accumulated. With Moseley’s (2016) interpretation, in which money costs are the same for values and prices, competition shifts around 29% of each country’s surplus value between industries (2014 median), and the effective rates by industry end up closer to one another than if each industry realised its own surplus value: equalisation exists, but it is partial. The temporal variant (TSSI) does not change the picture; it only raises the rate in years of inflation. The two agree on what matters for this reading: the rate of profit of the productive industries of the 43 countries rises until 2007 and falls thereafter (from 26.8% to 23.2% in 2014 with Moseley). Since version 3, education and health enter through their capitalist part, and they turn out to be among the industries that cede the most surplus value: their capital per worker is low.

The same logic operates between countries. Counting the labour contained in inputs, China and India each hand over, in 2014, more than 100 billion hours of labour more than they receive in their trade, and the high-income countries receive some 300 billion within the group of 43 countries. The OECD’s world tables, which value each imported input with the hours of the country that produced it, show the same pattern between 2000 and 2024, with a shift: the high-income countries receive between 230 and 390 billion hours a year; China, which handed over more than 200 billion in the mid-2000s, has handed over fewer than 80 since 2020, while India goes from 78 to more than 110. The transfer of labour through trade shifts with the relocation of production. And on the world market an hour of national labour is not worth the same everywhere, because the more productive national labour counts as more intensive labour (Marx, 2018, ch. XX): if each hour is weighted by the monetary productivity of its country, China’s share of the world mass of surplus labour falls from 33% to 18%, though it grows rapidly.

This sharpens the reading of the world mass. Measured in hours of exploited labour, the mass rests above all on China and India; measured in world-market hours, the centre retains a much greater weight, because its hours are worth more. The two measures are true at the same time: world capital reabsorbs population in the periphery, and the surplus value produced there is realised and redistributed, to a large extent, on the centre’s terms. The measurement is compatible with a theoretical reading that it does not demonstrate: that the limit capital faces is not the scarcity of exploitable living labour, but the growing requirement of capital and of appropriation to convert it into value that counts on the world market.

Ruptures: what the detector finds without looking for it

The break detector is given no date in advance. Even so, across all its configurations, it finds the same years again and again: 1975 is the most frequent break in three of the four configurations; 1958, 2004 and 2016–2018 appear in all of them, and 1994 in three of the four. The crisis of the mid-1970s, the recomposition of the 1990s and an inflection in the second half of the 2010s, followed by the robust rise of 2020–2025, are the tremors that the cognitive map records most regularly.

This does not by itself validate any periodisation. It does, however, offer a test for the hypothesis of stages of real subsumption: the breaks that the theory proposes can be compared with those that the detector finds without looking for them. The 2016–2018 inflection deserves particular attention, because it coincides with the expansion of the platform economy and with massive investment in artificial intelligence.

Artificial intelligence: appropriation at the centre of accumulation

Eight corporations —Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon, Meta, Nvidia, Oracle, Broadcom and AMD— appropriated in 2025 18.6% of US corporate profits and around 1.3% of the world mass of gross profit. Their operating profit is equivalent to 53% of their net fixed capital, and their investment to 37% of that capital in a single year and to 9.8% of all US private non-residential investment. Without them, the rate of the non-financial sector would be 19.8% instead of 22.5%.

These figures do not measure surplus value produced by these firms. They measure surplus value appropriated. Artificial intelligence today operates as a general condition of capitalist production (Dyer-Witheford, Kjøsen and Steinhoff, 2024, p. 55; Jiménez Cea, 2026), and whoever monopolises that condition —computing capacity, chips, models— intercepts a growing portion of the mass of surplus value produced elsewhere, just as landed property intercepts a growing part of surplus value without producing it (Marx, 2021, pp. 820–822). At the same time, the investment rate of these firms expresses the other side of the countertendency of the cheapening of constant capital: an accelerated accumulation at the highest point of the composition, which reduces the unit value of computing for the rest of capital and, at the same time, raises the mass of capital needed to compete. The extreme concentration of this industry is at once a result of the dynamics of the rate of profit and one of the means by which capital tries to counteract it.

Collapse? Kurz’s theory and the data

Robert Kurz’s theory of collapse is the most consistent theoretical formulation of the idea that the capitalist mode of production is heading towards an absolute internal limit that would lead it to irreversible collapse. The core of his theory of collapse can be summarised in three claims. First: the production of relative surplus value and the scientisation of production expel living labour from the immediate process of production, and this expulsion, beyond a certain threshold of the productive force, ceases to be relative and becomes absolute, so that the number of workers productive for capital diminishes. With this, the production of surplus value, and therefore the accumulation of capital, is desubstantialised. Second: with them the global mass of surplus value would diminish, because abstract labour, which Kurz understands as the expenditure of human energy, is the substance of value. Third: the microelectronic revolution, and all the more so the technologies that extend it, only accelerate this process; credit and fictitious capital postpone it without being able to reverse it (Kurz, 2012, pp. 300–312; see also Kurz, 2021).

Kurz’s three claims concern magnitudes, and this cognitive map measures those magnitudes. That a theory cannot be refuted with statistics, because its categories are not those of the national accounts, does not in any way exempt it from confronting critically the phenomenal manifestation of what it claims. What follows shows what form the movement the theory describes has taken in the available data.

Has the surplus-value-producing proletariat diminished?

No, neither in the United States nor on the world scale. In the United States, production and non-supervisory workers employed by productive capital go from 33.5 million in 1972 to 70.5 million in 2025 (not counting state employees or those of non-profit institutions). They grow not only in number, but also as a proportion of civilian employment, from 41% to 43%. In the 42 countries measured industry by industry, productive wage earners go from 767 million in 2000 to 1,077 million in 2025. Of those 42 countries, only two (Russia and Romania) have fewer productive workers today than in 2000.

The expulsion of the working class from the immediate process of commodity production that Kurz describes exists, but it has a precise location. In US manufacturing, production workers fall from 14.5 million in 1979 to 8.1 million in 2010, and barely recover afterwards (8.8 million in 2025). That absolute expulsion in industry is real. What is not borne out is its generalisation, since over the same period capital opened up or capitalised entire industries —transport and logistics, business services, for-profit health and education, leisure and hospitality— that absorbed more productive workers than manufacturing expelled. Furthermore, the expanded reproduction of world capital incorporated hundreds of millions of people in China, India and the rest of Asia into capitalist production. If productive labour is identified with industrial labour, as the theory of collapse of Kurz and his epigones tends to do, one of the forms of the expulsion of the working class from the immediate process of production is taken for its global result.

Is the global mass of surplus value diminishing?

Nor that. In the United States, the mass of surplus value of the business sector, in purchasing power, increases more than eightfold between 1948 and 2025, and the annual mass of surplus labour of productive workers goes from 42.5 billion hours in 1972 to 94.1 billion in 2025. On the world scale, the gross mass of surplus labour grows from 1,077 to 1,476 billion hours between 2000 and 2025.

There is, however, one datum that must be recorded with the same honesty. If the consumption of fixed capital is subtracted from surplus value, the net world mass of surplus labour grows until 2018 (1,261 billion hours) and has stagnated since then (1,265 billion in 2025). This is not a decline, and the estimate of depreciation for China and India is the most uncertain part of the whole measurement. Nor is it the first: after the 2008 crisis the net mass fell for three consecutive years and did not recover its 2007 level until 2012. But the current one is the longest stagnation of the quarter-century measured. This stagnation does not confirm an exhaustion of the substance of value; it does show that replacing the global mass of surplus value requires a growing portion of new labour, and that this requirement may come to absorb the growth.

Do the new technologies accelerate expulsion?

The period of the platform economy and of massive investment in artificial intelligence is still short, but it shows no acceleration of expulsion. Between 2018 and 2025, US productive workers increase by 6% and their mass of surplus labour by 7%. What the measurement does show is another movement: a handful of corporations that control computing, chips and models appropriate almost a fifth of US corporate profits. The new technology appears, for now, less as a mechanism of general expulsion than as a mechanism of monopolistic appropriation: a monopolised general condition of production, through which surplus value produced in other industries is redistributed.

Does increased exploitation compensate for the expulsion of workers?

The Capital & Class article (Jiménez Cea, 2026) argues that the expulsion of living labour can be compensated, within certain limits, by an increase in the degree of exploitation. This is Marx’s thesis in Volume III: the compensation for the decrease in the number of workers by the increase in the degree of exploitation runs up against insuperable limits, and for that reason it can check the fall in the rate ‘but not cancel it’ (Marx, 2021, p. 318). The data allow this question to be examined in three ways.

First, where productive workers diminish. Between 2000 and 2014 there are nine countries, out of the 42 economically largest countries analysed, in which the number of productive wage earners falls. In five of them the rate of surplus value rises enough to compensate wholly or almost wholly for the fall in the mass: Slovakia, Slovenia and the Netherlands end the period with more surplus labour than at the start, Romania loses 1% of its mass with 10% fewer workers, and Portugal 9% with 14% fewer. In the other four (Russia, Greece, Estonia and Denmark) the rate of surplus value also falls and the mass falls more than the number of workers. In the United States, in the years when productive employment fell, the compensation is partial and uneven: the mass falls less than the number of workers in some years (1975, 2002–2003, 2010, 2020; in 2020, mainly because of average hours), and not in others (1980, 1982, 1991, 2001, 2008, 2009). The compensation exists, but it is not automatic: it depends on capital managing to impose a higher rate of exploitation, and that is a result of struggle and of crisis, not a mechanical law.

Second, the limit of compensation. In the working day of the US productive worker, surplus labour today occupies 76% of the time (30.3 of 40 hours). Even if necessary labour could be reduced to almost nothing, surplus labour per worker could grow by only 32% with that working day. In other words: with the given working day, no increase in exploitation could compensate for a fall of more than 24% in the number of productive workers. In 1972 that margin was 35%. (The margin is the necessary part of the working day, 1/(1+e): it does not depend on how many hours the working day has.) This is not a distance to the limit of capital —the working day can be lengthened and intensified, and necessary labour never reaches zero— but the arithmetical form that Marx’s example takes in the given working day: two workers working twelve hours do not yield the surplus labour of twenty-four working two (Marx, 2018, pp. 372–373). That margin narrows as exploitation increases: it is an arithmetical property of the specific form of the capitalist working day, not a measure of how much capital has left before collapsing.

Third, what has actually sustained the mass. Compensation through an increase in the degree of exploitation has not, so far, been the main mechanism. In the United States, 94% of the growth in the mass of surplus labour between 1972 and 2025 is explained by the number of workers, and only 19% by the increase in the share of surplus labour in the working day (the reduction in average paid hours subtracts 12%). On the world scale, the rate of surplus value in hours does not rise (2.05 in 2000, 2.07 in 2025): the mass grows through proletarianisation. Where compensation through exploitation does prove decisive is in the rate of profit: between 1975 and 2004, the increase in the rate of surplus value counteracts the rising composition; between 2004 and 2016, when it stops rising, the rate of profit falls in all specifications.

What remains of the theory of collapse

The perspective of a general and absolute expulsion of the productive proletariat, and of an irreversibly diminishing mass of surplus value, finds no support in the available data. But the truth content of Kurz’s theory does not disappear on that account. The three paths by which capital has sustained its mass —incorporating new populations, opening up new industries, raising the degree of exploitation— each have their own limit: the exploitable population is not unlimited, the opening up of industries requires ever more capital per worker, and exploitation grows asymptotically. The net mass stagnating since 2018 and the fall in the rate between 2004 and 2016 are the manifestations of that growing requirement.

The difference from the theory of collapse lies not in denying the limits, but in their form. What the measurement shows is not a linear and irreversible exhaustion of the substance of value, but a social relation that reconstitutes itself again and again, on a broader basis and with greater violence, while undermining its own conditions. This reconstitution is not a destiny: it is the result of a contradictory social praxis, in which the same forces that capital mobilises to sustain itself are also those that could put an end to it.

Towards the field of countertendencies

The first measurement makes it possible to pose the question of the contemporary crisis more precisely. It is not a matter of how close capital is to a ceiling, but of how it articulates the field of its countertendencies today. The results suggest four features of the articulation of the countertendencies that Marx sets out (Marx, 2021, pp. 297–307), which the countertendencies module will have to test:

  1. The raising of the degree of exploitation has sustained the rate in the United States since 1982, but it operates in the asymptotic form that corresponds to a barrier that can only be strained: in the form of the working day, each compression of necessary labour yields proportionally less surplus labour, although the series do not yet show diminishing returns decade by decade.
  2. The cheapening of constant capital offset the rising composition between 1982 and 2000, but the composition rose again afterwards. Its effectiveness is not a tendency but a phase.
  3. The reabsorbed relative surplus population —the incorporation of new populations into productive labour, above all in China and India— is the main support of the world mass of surplus value. It is a countertendency that reinstates the barrier on an expanded scale: it needs ever more capital per worker and an exploitable population that is not unlimited.
  4. The transfer and anticipation of surplus value —the appropriation by monopolies of the general condition of production, foreign trade, interest-bearing capital and the secondary exploitation of households by finance— grow at the centre of the system without by themselves increasing the global mass of surplus value.

The decomposition by phases gives a first confirmation. Since 1972, in every phase the technical composition pushes the rate down, and since 1975 the relative cheapening of capital pushes it up without fully offsetting it; the raising of the rate of surplus value is the decisive counterweight between 1975 and 2004. Between 2004 and 2016, the only phase in which the rate of surplus value stops rising, the rate of profit falls. When a countertendency comes to a halt, the tendency reappears. The result does not depend on the theoretical decisions: between 2004 and 2016 the rate falls in all the business specifications of the multiverse, under both criteria of productive labour, and in all of them compensation through the rate of surplus value fails.

The other two features also already have a first manifestation. According to an approximation that does not yet trace intermediate inputs, the high-income countries have appropriated through foreign trade between 680 and 1,100 billion hours of others’ labour a year since 2005: for every dollar of trade they receive between three and four and a half times more hours than they hand over. China and India are the largest providers. And the market value of the equities of US non-financial corporations goes from 35% of GDP in 1982 to more than 200% in 2025, while their debt remains at around half of GDP: a growing mass of claims on surplus labour that has not yet been produced.

None of these features indicates exhaustion. They indicate an ever more demanding reconstitution of the capital relation, which needs to mobilise more capital, more population and more appropriation in order to sustain a growing mass of surplus value with a rate that fails to recover its post-war level in the broad measures of surplus value. This growing requirement, and the violence with which capital mobilises the entire field of its countertendencies to meet it, is what the research seeks to make visible.

What this measurement cannot yet say. It does not measure the intensity of labour or the extension of the working day beyond paid hours; it separates the public and non-profit part of education and health using approximate country proportions; it measures the surplus by industry after 2014 only for the OECD, Brazil and India (2015–2020) and China (2015–2022, with OECD tables that do not separate China’s employees); and it works with monetary magnitudes, not with labour values calculated from input–output matrices. Each of these limitations is documented in the methodology.
